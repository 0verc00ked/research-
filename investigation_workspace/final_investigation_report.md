# Foreign Private Equity and Institutional Capital in India's Healthcare, Pharmaceutical, Food, and Agriculture Systems

Report date: June 4, 2026

## 1. Executive Summary

This investigation reviewed the repository's reports, charts, PDF, and extracted source artifacts, then independently checked the central claims against official policy documents, CCI approvals and orders, company disclosures, industry data, and peer-reviewed literature. The final finding is not that every claim in the repository is proven. The accurate finding is narrower and stronger: foreign PE and institutional capital have become structurally important in several Indian healthcare, pharmaceutical, medical-device, packaged-food, and agriculture-input assets; India's FDI and competition framework has generally permitted these transactions; international evidence shows credible risks from PE ownership in healthcare; but India-specific causal evidence on prices, quality, access, food prices, nutrition, and farmer outcomes is incomplete.

The strongest verified evidence is transaction-level. Carlyle invested about US$490 million for 20% of Piramal Pharma in 2020 [S20]. Temasek-linked entities became the dominant foreign shareholder group in Manipal Health Enterprises after the 2023 transaction, with Temasek acquiring an additional 41%, Sheares holding 18%, TPG reinvesting 11%, and the Manipal Group retaining about 30% [S21]. Manipal's 2026 SEBI abridged prospectus then provides a current filing baseline: promoters totalled 69.86% pre-offer and the company operated 48 hospitals with 12,367 licensed beds as of September 30, 2025 [S117]. Blackstone- and TPG-backed Quality Care India Limited, which operates CARE, KIMS Health, and Evercare brands, received CCI approval for its merger into Aster DM Healthcare; the CCI press release identified BCP Asia as Blackstone-owned and Centella as TPG-controlled, and described QCIL as operating 26 healthcare centers with 5,150+ beds [S10]. Aster's scanned QCIL shareholding pattern verifies BCP Asia II Topco IV Pte. Ltd. as promoter with 71.84% before the merger process [S119]. KKR acquired or agreed to acquire controlling positions in Healthium MedTech, Baby Memorial Hospital, and HCG [S13, S14, S22, S24]. Advent exited Bharat Serums and Vaccines to Mankind [S23]. KKR-linked Tau agreed to sell control of JB Chemicals to Torrent, and CCI approved the transaction only after voluntary modifications addressing prima facie competition concerns in specific finished-dosage-form markets [S15, S16].

Additional historical and recent control-level transactions extend the picture across hospitals, single-specialty platforms, pharma and healthcare-services. KKR-backed Radiant Life Care acquired Life Healthcare's 49.7% Max Healthcare stake in December 2018 and reverse-merged into Max Healthcare to operate over 3,200 beds across 16 hospitals [S146]; KKR has since dispersed that listed position so that Max Healthcare's March 2026 BSE pattern shows promoter group at 23.71% and FPI/MF-dominated public float of 76.29% [S108, CO033]. In single-specialty care, TPG Growth and GIC co-control Asia Healthcare Holdings, which combines Motherhood (24 hospitals, 12 cities), Nova IVF (95 IVF centres, 57 cities) and AINU (7 hospitals, 5 cities) and previously built and exited CTSI in 2019 [S153, S157]. Manipal Health Enterprises also closed Columbia Asia India in May 2021 for Rs 2,100 crore and 11 hospitals [S148]. Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan Board completed its first control PE buyout in India in 2022 by acquiring a significant majority of Sahyadri Hospitals from Everstone, with 8 hospitals and roughly 900 beds [S155]. Carlyle acquired up to 74% of SeQuent Scientific, India's largest pure-play animal-health company, at INR 86 per share in May 2020 [S147]. KKR exited Gland Pharma in October 2017 when Shanghai Fosun Pharmaceutical acquired about 74% at US$1.09 billion [S145]. Bain Capital bought Blackstone's Emcure Pharmaceuticals stake in December 2013, exemplifying secondary PE-to-PE pharma transfers [S144]. CVC Capital's Sajjan India (now Cohizon Life Sciences) buyout in 2021 at about Rs 7,000 crore valuation and 89% stake widens the foreign-PE footprint in pharma/specialty-chemicals CDMOs [S150]. The healthcare BPO/RCM line is also institutionally controlled: BPEA bought the HGS healthcare BPO business at US$1.2 billion in 2021 [S149], EQT then acquired BPEA, and the rebranded Sagility India listed in November 2024 through a Rs 2,106.40 crore OFS [S156]. Sapphire Foods India's November 2021 IPO at Rs 1,180 per share crystallised significant Goldman Sachs (18.4%), Edelweiss (9.4%) and CX Partners (6.5%) holdings in India's largest Yum Brands franchisee [S151].

Aggregate healthcare-investment figures are now better triangulated but still secondary. Dealflow IQ's 2025 India hospitals report states that healthcare PE/VC inflows exceeded US$15.5 billion over the prior five years, while hospitals attracted US$4.96 billion in PE and US$6.74 billion in M&A deals between 2022 and 2024 [S130]. EY-IVCA separately reports US$14.5 billion of healthcare PE/VC investment in India since 2020, with 58% in 2023 and 2024 and hospitals/clinics accounting for 60% [S140]. Grant Thornton/AHPI reports that healthcare and pharma recorded 594 major M&A and PE deals worth over US$30 billion in 2022-2024, with hospitals accounting for about 40% of total deal value [S139]. Bain-IVCA gives the broader market frame: India PE-VC investment recovered to about US$43 billion in 2024, including about US$29 billion of PE and US$14 billion of VC/growth investment, while healthcare deal volumes rose about 80% [S141]. These sources support the scale finding, but they mix PE, VC, M&A, sovereign/institutional and strategic capital and are not official statistics or independently rebuilt deal ledgers. IMARC's public market-research page gives the repository's hospital-market forecast of US$193.42 billion in 2025 and US$364.55 billion by 2034 at 7.30% CAGR [S131], while ICRA provides a capacity benchmark: private hospitals account for about 59-60% of available beds and an 18-large-chain sample is expected to add more than 34,000 beds during FY2026-FY2030 [S136].

The strongest food and agriculture evidence is also transaction-level, not impact-level. CCI approved Temasek/Jongsong's less-than-10% investment in Haldiram Snacks Food [S17], Alpha Wave/IHC's 6% investment [S18], and L Catterton India Fund's minority investment plus an advisory agreement under which an L Catterton adviser may receive commercially sensitive information and advise Haldiram on strategy, talent, and performance [S19]. This is meaningful institutional-capital influence. It is not, on current evidence, proof of foreign control. In agriculture inputs, UPL disclosed that Alpha Wave would invest US$350 million for approximately 12.5% of Advanta Enterprises, following a prior US$300 million KKR investment in October 2022 [S26]. NABARD and MoSPI/NSO now provide baseline rural and agricultural-household data for testing farmer-impact claims, including income, expenditure, food-share, debt, landholding, agricultural-household income composition and crop-sale channels [S142, S143]. They do not prove downstream farmer effects from PE ownership.

The policy evidence shows a permissive and incentive-rich investment environment. DPIIT's FDI policy allows 100% automatic-route FDI in manufacturing; food-product retail trading, including e-commerce, for food manufactured or produced in India is 100% permitted under government approval route; pharma is 100% automatic for greenfield and 74% automatic for brownfield, with government approval beyond 74%; and medical devices manufacturing is 100% automatic [S01, S04]. NITI Aayog's healthcare investment report states that the hospital sector and medical devices allow 100% FDI under the automatic route, while healthcare/pharma brownfield investment is more restricted above 74% [S05]. MoFPI's food-processing PLI and PMKSY evidence shows public support for scale manufacturing, cold-chain and food-processing infrastructure [S89-S92], and Income Tax Department guidance identifies cold-chain facilities, agri warehousing and hospitals with at least 100 beds as Section 35AD specified businesses [S93]. The repository's broad claim that "food and beverage" was newly opened to 100% FDI in January 2025 should be corrected.

The health-financing context is serious but must be stated accurately. The latest official National Health Accounts release for 2022-23 puts government health expenditure at 1.43% of GDP under the old GDP series, or 1.48% under the new GDP series, and out-of-pocket expenditure at 43.4% of total health expenditure [S08]. The National Health Policy 2017 target was to raise government health expenditure to 2.5% of GDP by 2025 [S06, S07]. Thus, the public-spending target remains unmet in the latest official data. But the repository's 54.8% current-OOPE figure is outdated and should not be used as the current official estimate.

Public insurance evidence is now stronger. PIB/MoHFW reports that AB-PMJAY covers 12 crore families, had created about 42.48 crore Ayushman cards by 1 December 2025, and had authorized 10.98 crore admissions worth Rs 1.60 lakh crore by that date [S75]. Official sources also report 15,532 private hospitals empanelled as of 1 December 2025 and more than 15,733 private hospitals as of 31 December 2025 [S74, S75]. Peer-reviewed literature supports the repository's older rough 20% participation frame only with date and denominator caveats: BMC cites 43,487 estimated private hospitals and fewer than 9000 PM-JAY-empanelled private hospitals, while PLOS One reports March 2020 PM-JAY empanelment of 20,257 total hospitals, including 8,157 private for-profit and 733 private not-for-profit facilities [S137, S138]. BMC's 2023-24 Maharashtra case study also found only 13% private-hospital empanelment and few large private hospitals participating [S137]. This verifies uneven private-sector participation over time, but it still does not show chain-level participation by PE-backed premium hospitals or prove denial, balance-billing, or restriction behavior.

Food-system comparator evidence now reduces confirmation bias. US packaged consumer-product evidence suggests PE effects can appear as product launches, premiumization, and geographic expansion as much as direct same-product price increases [S83, S84]. Supermarket LBO evidence shows pricing effects depend on local competition and rival leverage [S85], while restaurant-chain PE evidence reports improved health inspections and lower menu prices but lower headcount [S86]. These comparators support mechanism-testing for India; they do not prove Indian food-price, farmer-income, or nutrition harms.

Additional verification has strengthened the historical and platform map. Warburg Pincus's 2005 Max Healthcare investment is verified by Max India's own release: Rs 140 crore in Max Healthcare for a 23% fully diluted stake and Rs 340 crore total Max group exposure [S32]. Apax's Apollo Hospitals portfolio page verifies a 2007 realised investment [S33]. TPG's 2015 Manipal release verifies a Rs 900 crore significant minority investment [S34]. Diagnostics/digital health is now represented by API/PharmEasy's Thyrocare control acquisition, Carlyle-Metropolis, WestBridge-Dr Lal, and the CCI-approved Fortis/Agilus consolidation [S35, S44, S45, S111]. Insurance/claims administration is represented by Star Health and Medi Assist filings plus IRDAI 2024-25 claims-flow and TPA network data [S47, S48, S62, S79-S81, S94, S103, S112]. Food distribution and quick commerce are represented by Swiggy's SEBI prospectus and official March 2026 BSE shareholding pattern [S36, S107], Zomato-Blinkit [S37], Tata-BigBasket [S38, S39], and Reliance-METRO India [S40, S41].

International healthcare evidence supports regulatory caution. A BMJ systematic review found that PE ownership was often associated with higher costs and mixed-to-worse quality outcomes across international healthcare studies [S27]. A JAMA study of US Medicare hospitalizations found a 25.4% increase in hospital-acquired adverse events after PE acquisition compared with matched non-PE hospitals [S28]. These are strong risk signals, but they are not direct proof that PE ownership has worsened outcomes in India. India-specific literature more strongly supports a mechanism of financialisation, corporatization, and investor-driven restructuring than it proves nationwide causal effects on patient outcomes [S29].

The repository's most serious weakness is causal overreach. It often moves from "foreign PE invested in hospitals" to "foreign PE caused overcharging, denial of care, and regulatory capture." The first proposition is verified for many assets. The second is plausible in some mechanisms and supported by international analogues, but not yet proven at national scale in India. NPPA's Fortis Memorial Research Institute Gurgaon tables verify extreme corporate-hospital billing-rate/procurement margins, including 1,737% on a three-way stop cock, 914% on a non-scheduled formulation, and 343% on a scheduled formulation [S31]. That is evidence of corporate-hospital pricing practices and regulatory concern; it is not, by itself, PE-specific evidence.

Bottom line: the investigation supports a parliamentary or antitrust inquiry into healthcare-specific ownership transparency, public-interest review of healthcare M&A, patient-price monitoring, PMJAY participation, debt/value-extraction practices, and food-sector concentration. It does not support making allegations of corruption, national takeover, or proven farmer harm without more primary evidence.

## 2. Methodology

The investigation used a two-layer method.

First, repository materials were treated as a claim inventory, not as evidence. The local corpus consists of generated markdown reports, a generated PDF, and generated charts. These artifacts are useful for identifying claims and search leads, but they repeatedly use loaded language such as "takeover," "corruption," and "extractive by design" before proving the causal chain. The image inventory in `image_inventory.csv` classifies all charts as generated infographics requiring underlying-data verification, and `repository_claim_database.csv` separates 71 recurring repository assertions from verified findings.

Second, high-impact claims were checked against stronger evidence: DPIIT policy documents and FDI fact sheets, NITI Aayog's healthcare investment report, National Health Policy 2017, official NHA data releases, PM-JAY releases, MoFPI incentive-policy releases, Income Tax Department guidance, CCI press releases and orders, company disclosures, EY/EY-IVCA, Grant Thornton/AHPI, Bain-IVCA and Dealflow IQ market-intelligence sources, IRDAI insurance data, and peer-reviewed academic literature. `current_ownership_review.csv`, `health_policy_regulation_review.csv`, `insurance_tpa_review.csv`, `policy_incentives_review.csv`, `concentration_screening.csv`, and `impact_causality_matrix.csv` provide the supporting audit trail for ownership, public-insurance/regulatory, insurance/TPA, incentive-policy, antitrust-screening, and causal claims. Every major conclusion below is classified using the required evidence labels.

The investigation separates five kinds of claims:

| Classification | Meaning |
|---|---|
| VERIFIED FACT | Directly supported by official, company, regulatory, or peer-reviewed source. |
| STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION | Supported by multiple credible sources, but with some inference or non-finality. |
| EXPERT INTERPRETATION | Reasoned synthesis from evidence, but not directly observable as a fact. |
| PLAUSIBLE HYPOTHESIS | Mechanistically plausible and partially supported, but needs stronger data. |
| UNPROVEN CLAIM | Insufficient evidence or overstatement. |

## 3. Repository Review Findings

### 3.1 What the Repository Gets Right

**VERIFIED FACT:** The repository correctly identifies a major expansion of foreign institutional ownership in Indian healthcare and life sciences. Multiple post-2020 transactions are verified by CCI and company disclosures: Blackstone/QCIL/Aster, Temasek/Manipal, KKR/HCG, KKR/Healthium, KKR/Baby Memorial, Carlyle/Piramal Pharma, Advent/BSV, and KKR/JB/Torrent [S10-S24].

**VERIFIED FACT:** The repository correctly identifies Haldiram as a major packaged-food asset attracting multiple foreign institutional investors. CCI approved less-than-10% Temasek/Jongsong, 6% Alpha Wave/IHC, and a minority L Catterton investment [S17-S19].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** The repository correctly flags public-health financing as a core context. NHP 2017 set a 2.5% GDP public-health-expenditure target, and the latest official NHA data remain below that target [S06-S08].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** The repository correctly flags international healthcare PE literature as relevant. BMJ and JAMA evidence supports concerns about cost and quality risks under PE ownership [S27, S28].

### 3.2 What the Repository Overstates or Gets Wrong

**UNPROVEN CLAIM:** "Foreign PE has taken over India's healthcare and food systems." Major assets are foreign-backed, but national ownership concentration is not fully measured. Organized hospital-bed share, specialty-level market shares, pharma segment shares, and food-category concentration still need verified datasets.

**UNPROVEN CLAIM:** "PE caused India's widening private-public hospital cost gap." The correlation is plausible, but the private-public cost gap is also shaped by technology intensity, public-sector rationing, location, case mix, labor, real estate, insurance, and medical inflation. Facility-level before/after data are needed.

**UNPROVEN CLAIM:** "PE ownership has already raised Indian food prices or harmed farmers." The current evidence verifies ownership changes, not downstream food-price, nutrition, procurement, or farmer-income outcomes.

**Correction:** The latest official NHA 2022-23 OOPE figure is 43.4%, not 54.8% [S08].

**Correction:** Food-sector FDI must be described carefully. Manufacturing is 100% automatic; food-product retail trading for food manufactured/produced in India is 100% under government approval route [S01, S04].

**Correction:** The repository's antibiotic chart should be narrowed. PNAS verifies India's antibiotic consumption increased from 3.2 to 6.5 billion DDDs from 2000 to 2015, but the "highest in the world" wording requires class/metric qualification and is not PE-specific evidence [S120].

**Correction:** The stent-price-cap chart should also be narrowed. Official NPPA and parliamentary sources verify billing-detail non-compliance, trade-margin limits, non-stent-component disclosure requirements, and a transparency/enforcement response after the stent price cap. They do not prove a quantified, systemwide shift into procedure fees; a 2019 parliamentary answer says reviewed stent-overcharging complaints usually found no direct stent overcharging [S121-S124].

## 5. Healthcare Sector Findings

### 5.1 Hospitals

**VERIFIED FACT:** Blackstone and TPG-backed QCIL is a major hospital platform. CCI states that QCIL operates CARE Hospitals, KIMS Health, and Evercare brands, with 26 healthcare centers and 5,150+ beds across 14 cities at the time of the Aster-QCIL approval [S10]. Aster's scanned QCIL shareholding pattern shows BCP Asia II Topco IV Pte. Ltd. as the only promoter/promoter-group shareholder, holding 273,663,300 shares or 71.84%; public shareholders held 28.16% [S119]. Aster's announcement described the combined Aster-QCIL platform as 38 hospitals and 10,150+ beds, with post-merger Aster shareholders at 57.3%, QCIL shareholders at 42.7%, Aster promoters at 24.0%, and Blackstone at 30.7% [S11]. Subsequent Aster/NSE materials show shareholder approval and a Q1 FY27 completion target, while the April 2, 2026 intimation states that the joint company petition was fixed for NCLT hearing on May 22, 2026; no final effective-date filing was identified in the reviewed official materials as of June 4, 2026 [S57, S118].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Temasek-linked entities acquired a dominant institutional position in Manipal Health Enterprises. The Manipal release states Temasek would acquire an additional 41%, Sheares already held 18%, TPG would reinvest 11%, and Manipal Group would hold about 30% [S21]. The 2026 SEBI abridged prospectus provides a current operating and promoter baseline: Manipal operated 48 hospitals, including six O&M hospitals, with 12,367 licensed beds as of September 30, 2025; pre-offer promoter holdings totalled 69.86%, including Kangto 26.45%, Imperius 17.77%, Kabru 5.78%, Manipal Global Health Services 19.68%, and Dr. Pai 0.18%; and promoter entities include Mauritius and Singapore investment holding companies [S117].

**VERIFIED FACT:** KKR's HCG deal is a control transaction. CCI states that Hector and EBT proposed to acquire up to 54% of HCG from Aceso in two tranches, with a mandatory tender offer for up to 26%, producing an expected final stake between 54% and 77% [S13]. Official BSE March 2026 data show Hector Asia Holdings II Pte. Ltd. held 54.27% as foreign promoter, with total promoter and promoter group at 64.21%, public shareholding at 35.79%, and grand total shares of 14,93,02,203 [S104].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** KKR is pursuing hospital-platform expansion, not passive exposure only. The HCG release frames KKR as assuming sole operational control, BSE's March 2026 HCG note records promoter-agreement restrictions and right-of-first-offer context involving Hector/KIA EBT and the BSA promoter group, and the Baby Memorial release frames KKR's role as supporting organic and inorganic expansion [S14, S24, S104].

**VERIFIED FACT:** The early entry narrative is partly verified. Warburg Pincus invested in Max Healthcare in 2005 [S32], Apax invested in Apollo Hospitals in 2007 [S33], and TPG invested Rs 900 crore in Manipal in 2015 [S34]. KKR's hospital control history is now also primary-sourced: KKR-backed Radiant Life Care acquired Life Healthcare's 49.7% Max Healthcare stake in an all-cash deal in December 2018 and reverse-merged its assets into Max Healthcare, with KKR and Abhay Soi as co-promoters of a 3,200-bed, 16-hospital combined entity [S146]. Current ownership must be treated separately from historical PE entry: official BSE March 2026 data for Max Healthcare show promoter group 23.71%, public shareholders 76.29%, FPIs 45.39%, mutual funds 16.30%, insurance companies 6.31%, provident/pension funds 3.43%, and foreign-ownership utilization of 45.49%, but do not show current PE control [S108]. Manipal also completed Columbia Asia India in May 2021 for Rs 2,100 crore and 11 hospitals, making Manipal India's then-second-largest multi-specialty chain at about 7,200 beds [S148].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Foreign PE/sovereign control extends beyond multi-specialty chains into single-specialty roll-ups and pension-PE buyouts. TPG Growth incubated Asia Healthcare Holdings (AHH) in 2016 and Singapore's GIC entered AHH with US$170 million in February 2022 and an additional US$150 million in December 2024 for combined funding of about US$320 million. AHH operates Motherhood (24 women and children's hospitals across 12 cities), Nova IVF (95 IVF centres across 57 cities in India and South Asia) and AINU (7 urology/nephrology hospitals across 5 cities); it previously built and exited CTSI, India's second-largest oncology chain, in 2019 [S153, S157]. Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan Board announced its first control PE buyout in India in August 2022, acquiring a significant majority of Sahyadri Hospitals Group from Everstone; Sahyadri then operated 8 hospitals with about 900 operating beds, 300 critical-care beds, 2,000 clinicians and 2,600 supporting staff [S155]. These transactions show that institutional capital in Indian hospitals now spans multi-specialty chains, single-specialty platforms and pension-PE direct ownership.

**OPEN EVIDENCE GAP:** No complete national map of PE-controlled hospital beds has been verified. Reviewed PE/institutional platforms total about 26,227 beds, or roughly 1.39% of a NITI/World Bank all-India bed proxy and roughly 2.3% of an implied private-bed proxy using ICRA's 59-60% private-bed share [S05, S117, S136]. Against a narrow large-chain denominator, the same beds may be material: ICRA's 18-large-chain sample implies roughly 68,000-71,000 existing beds before planned additions [S136]. But that is not a complete organized-bed denominator and is not PE ownership-tagged. The repository's high PE-bed-share estimates should not be used without a bed-level data table and ownership thresholds.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Small-provider displacement is a real contextual issue, but causation is multi-factor. Hindustan Times reported in 2012 that the Bombay Nursing Homes Association said about 20% of 650-odd registered Mumbai nursing homes had shut down, mostly in South Mumbai [S132]. The later India financialisation study uses this as part of a wider account of small hospitals and nursing homes under pressure from land costs, technology, staffing, insurance reimbursement, regulations, and competition with corporate chains [S29]. This supports a corporatization/displacement concern, but not a finding that PE ownership caused those closures.

### 5.2 Diagnostics, Imaging, and Digital Health

**VERIFIED FACT:** API Holdings/PharmEasy acquired control of Thyrocare, a listed diagnostics chain. The SEBI detailed public statement records a share-purchase agreement for 66.11% of Thyrocare and a 26% mandatory open offer [S35]. It also identifies API Holdings shareholders including Naspers Ventures, MacRitchie Investments, Evermed Holdings, and TPG Growth [S35].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Carlyle invested in Metropolis Healthcare in 2015; Carlyle's release described Metropolis as having 130 laboratories and more than 1,000 collection centers in India [S44]. WestBridge's portfolio page identifies a 2005 Series A investment in Dr Lal PathLabs and describes Dr Lal as offering more than 5,000 tests across pathology and radiology [S45]. Current listed ownership should be separated from those historical entries: BSE March 2026 data show Metropolis at 48.87% promoter/group and 51.13% public, with mutual funds at 33.28% and FPIs at 10.43% [S109]; Dr Lal at 53.21% promoter/group and 46.44% public, with domestic institutions at 21.38% and FPIs at 17.18% [S110]. The current BSE patterns do not show current PE control for either listed diagnostics chain.

**VERIFIED FACT:** Agilus/SRL ownership is partly clarified by the official CCI/PIB record for Fortis Healthcare's acquisition of additional Agilus share capital. CCI approved Fortis acquiring an additional 31.52% of Agilus, raising Fortis's stake from 57.68% to 89.2% post combination; CCI also describes Fortis's diagnostics business as primarily through Agilus [S111]. This supports a strategic-consolidation reading, not current PE control. Completion evidence, named sellers, residual minority holders, shareholder agreements, and the IHH/Fortis control chain remain follow-up items.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Insurance and TPA exposure is now better grounded but still not causally resolved. Star Health's DRHP documents investor transactions involving WestBridge, Madison, Apis, US entities, Tata Capital, and IRDAI approvals [S47]. Star Health's Q3 FY26 public disclosure gives a primary December 2025 ownership benchmark: Safecrop Investments India LLP 40.19%, late Rakesh Jhunjhunwala 1.55%, Rekha Jhunjhunwala 15.57%, mutual funds 12.95%, FPIs 12.20%, insurance companies 5.03%, AIFs 2.98%, FDI 1.87%, and stated foreign shareholding of 14.50% [S94]. Official BSE March 2026 shareholding data report promoter and promoter group 57.98%, public shareholders 42.02%, FPIs 15.05%, FDI 1.87%, mutual funds 11.93%, AIFs 3.16%, insurance companies 5.19%, non-institution public subtotal 6.66%, and grand total 58,84,03,502 shares; BSE notes Jhunjhunwala transmission complexity and an April 6, 2026 IRDAI approval reference [S103]. Medi Assist's IPO public announcement identifies Bessemer Health and Investcorp Private Equity Fund I as major selling shareholders in a TPA/health-benefits administrator [S48]. Official BSE March 2026 Medi Assist data show promoter/group 4.61%, public shareholders 95.39%, mutual funds 36.39%, domestic institutions 49.19%, FPIs 24.32%, foreign companies 3.41% including Novo Holdings A/S, and foreign-ownership limit utilized 28.40%; BSE notes exchange no-objection letters reclassifying Bessemer-linked entities and others from promoter/promoter group to public effective April 2, 2026 [S112].

**VERIFIED FACT:** TPAs are operationally important in health-claim settlement. IRDAI's 2024-25 annual report states that general and health insurers settled 3.26 crore health insurance claims and paid Rs 94,248 crore, and that 69% of settled claim counts were through TPAs versus 31% in-house [S79]. IRDAI also reports Medi Assist Insurance TPA had 20,204 network agreements as of 31 March 2025 and that Raksha Health Insurance TPA merged with Medi Assist effective 1 January 2025 [S79]. This makes TPA consolidation and ownership relevant to affordability and access analysis, but it does not prove investor-caused claim denials, deductions, or premium changes.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Standalone health insurance is concentrated by premium, while TPA network agreements are more dispersed. IRDAI Statement 8 reports FY 2024-25 standalone-health-insurer health+PA+travel gross direct premium of Rs 38,413.57 crore, of which Star Health contributed Rs 16,716.20 crore, or about 43.5%; a simple premium-share HHI for standalone health insurers is about 2,849 [S79]. By contrast, IRDAI's TPA network-agreement table reports 2,73,816 total agreements, with Medi Assist at 20,204, or about 7.4%; a simple network-agreement HHI is about 712 [S79]. These are concentration proxies, not proof of ownership-caused claim behavior.

**PLAUSIBLE HYPOTHESIS:** Institutional ownership in insurers or TPAs may affect claim handling, network bargaining, pre-authorization, package rates, and settlement deductions. The mechanism is plausible because TPAs handle most settled claim counts, but the current evidence does not prove ownership-caused denials, premium increases, or worse settlement outcomes.

**PLAUSIBLE HYPOTHESIS:** Diagnostics and digital health are attractive to PE/VC because of scale economies, network effects, preventive-health demand, insurance/consumer-pay demand, and workflow automation opportunities. NITI Aayog identifies diagnostics, telemedicine, and digital health as investment opportunities [S05].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Digital-health and healthcare-services exposure is verified beyond PharmEasy/Thyrocare. Infinx announced that a KKR-managed fund would invest in Infinx Services Private Limited, an AI-driven healthcare revenue-cycle-management provider serving the US healthcare market; Norwest also participated, and transaction terms were undisclosed [S100]. Apollo HealthCo/Apollo 24/7 agreed to raise INR 2,475 crore from Advent for 12.1% in the merged Apollo HealthCo/Keimed entity, while AHEL would remain the largest controlling shareholder with at least 59.2%; PIB/CCI later approved the Advent/Rasmeli, Apollo HealthCo and Keimed combination [S101, S102]. However, this package still does not fully verify current foreign-PE ownership across all imaging networks, health-tech platforms, and insurance intermediaries.

### 5.3 Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices

**VERIFIED FACT:** Carlyle's 2020 Piramal Pharma investment was for 20%, with an estimated equity investment of about US$490 million [S20].

**VERIFIED FACT:** KKR's Healthium transaction transferred a controlling interest in a major medical-device platform from Apax funds to KKR [S22]. EY identifies KKR's acquisition of Healthium Medtech for US$838 million as a top healthcare deal in 2024 [S09].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Advent exited BSV to Mankind. Mankind announced the BSV acquisition at about INR 13,630 crore enterprise value and later announced completion with INR 13,768 crore purchase consideration [S23].

**VERIFIED FACT:** CCI's Torrent-JB order shows that pharma consolidation can raise product-level concerns. CCI formed a prima facie view of likely AAEC in certain FDF markets, then approved the transaction subject to remedies: five-year licensing of Vizylac Lactobacillus Acidophilus single-strain FDF, divestiture of Calcigard Nifedipine products, and three-year price cap/continued marketing for Azovas [S15].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Pharma PE history extends well before the 2020-2025 wave. Blackstone exited Emcure Pharmaceuticals to Bain Capital in December 2013; Emcure was India's 14th-largest pharma company by market share at the time [S144]. KKR exited Gland Pharma in October 2017 when Shanghai Fosun Pharmaceutical completed acquisition of about 74% at about US$1.09 billion; KKR had held about 38.4% since 2014, and the Penmetsa family retained operational roles [S145]. Carlyle expanded into animal health by agreeing to acquire up to 74% of SeQuent Scientific in May 2020 at INR 86 per share via SPAs and an open offer; SeQuent was India's largest pure-play animal-healthcare company with manufacturing in India, Spain, Turkey, Germany and Brazil [S147]. CVC Capital acquired about 89% of Sajjan India in September 2021 at about Rs 7,000 crore valuation, an Indian CDMO covering APIs, intermediates and specialty chemicals (now Cohizon Life Sciences) [S150]. Each of these is verified by company release, regulator filing or deal-database confirmation; collectively they show that foreign PE engagement in Indian pharma has been multi-cycle and includes secondary PE-to-PE transfers (Blackstone-to-Bain), PE-to-strategic exits (KKR-to-Fosun, Advent-to-Mankind), and direct control buyouts (Carlyle, CVC).

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Healthcare BPO/RCM has become an institutionally controlled segment that interacts with the Indian healthcare workforce even when the customer base is offshore. Hinduja Global Solutions announced sale of its healthcare BPO business to BPEA Fund VIII (via Betaine B.V.) at a US$1.2 billion enterprise value in August 2021, with the business reporting over 20,000 employees across 34 delivery centers [S149]. After EQT's 2022 acquisition of BPEA, the platform was renamed Sagility and IPO'd on Indian exchanges in November 2024 in an OFS-only IPO of 70.22 crore shares totalling Rs 2,106.40 crore [S156]. The investor pipeline therefore moved from strategic Indian corporate (HGS) through PE (BPEA) to listed PE-controlled (EQT/Sagility) within three years.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** The pharma and medical-device story is not simply "foreign control." It includes PE exits to Indian strategics, foreign minority stakes, buyouts, and CCI product-level merger review. The Torrent-JB order is evidence that CCI can scrutinize pharma overlaps more granularly than the repository implies [S15].

## 6. Food, Agriculture, and Nutrition Findings

### 6.1 Packaged Foods

**VERIFIED FACT:** Haldiram Snacks Food has attracted multiple foreign/institutional investors. CCI approved a less-than-10% acquisition by Jongsong/Temasek [S17], a 6% acquisition by Alpha Wave/IHC [S18], and a minority acquisition by L Catterton India Fund [S19]. Financial Express reports the Temasek transaction at about Rs 8,600 crore / US$1 billion and about Rs 86,000 crore / US$10 billion valuation; this is valuation context, not an official CCI-established figure [S133]. A PRNewswire/company release verifies L Catterton's strategic partnership and investment, including brand, product, supply-chain, distribution, geographic-expansion, and talent-development value-creation areas, but it does not disclose percentage or price [S134].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Haldiram Snacks Food is a consolidated packaged-food vehicle. The Alpha Wave order states the target would house the FMCG businesses of Haldiram Snacks Private Limited and Haldiram Foods International Private Limited, with pre-transaction target shareholding jointly held by HSPL shareholders at 56% and HFIPL shareholders at 44% [S18].

**VERIFIED FACT:** The L Catterton order identifies a governance influence channel. It states that L Catterton Mumbai Advisory Services may receive commercially sensitive information and may advise Haldiram on strategy, talent, and performance [S19].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Additional packaged-food and QSR assets also show institutional-capital entry. CCI approved General Atlantic Singapore BWP's acquisition of a 6.93% fully diluted stake in Balaji Wafers [S70], and General Atlantic/Balaji described the deal as strategic growth capital with financial terms undisclosed [S71]. Economic Times reports prior ET terms of 7% for Rs 2,500 crore at about Rs 35,000 crore / almost US$4 billion valuation; this valuation remains press-supported, not official [S135]. CCI's detailed Theobroma order verifies a ChrysCapital-related proposed acquisition of approximately 84% of Theobroma's shareholding and voting rights on a fully diluted basis [S72].

**UNPROVEN CLAIM:** These minority stakes do not establish foreign control of Haldiram. CCI's public orders describe minority shareholding and no AAEC finding, not control transfer [S17-S19].

### 6.2 Food Distribution and Quick Commerce

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Haldiram's channel exposure includes modern digital distribution. CCI's L Catterton order states the target sells through distributors/stockists and channels including e-commerce platforms, quick commerce platforms, retail kirana stores, and others [S19].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Food and grocery demand aggregation is increasingly platformized. Swiggy's prospectus identifies a food-delivery and quick-commerce business with major foreign institutional investors and no identifiable promoter [S36]. Official BSE March 2026 data verify no promoter holding, public shareholders at 94.41%, an employee stock option trust at 5.59%, foreign-ownership utilization of 52.19%, MIH India Food Holdings BV at 21.06%, SVF II Songbird at 6.26%, Accel India IV Mauritius at 2.77%, Tencent Cloud Europe at 1.97%, Elevation Capital V at 1.35%, and Norwest Venture Partners VII-A Mauritius at 1.33% [S107]. Zomato's Blinkit filing records proposed acquisition consideration of INR 45,082 million and describes quick commerce as extending beyond grocery into multiple categories [S37]. CCI approved Tata Digital's acquisition of up to 64.3% of BigBasket's B2B entity and sole control over its B2C entity [S38]. Reliance Retail announced acquisition of 100% of METRO Cash & Carry India for Rs 2,850 crore, adding 31 large-format stores across 21 cities and a large B2B/kirana customer base [S40, S41]. Zepto is now also in the evidence base through a company-quoted press report of an approximately US$450 million CalPERS-led funding round at a US$7 billion valuation [S73].

**EXPERT INTERPRETATION:** These distribution cases matter for food-system bargaining power, data access, channel dependence, and private-label/assortment decisions. But Zomato-Blinkit, Tata-BigBasket, and Reliance-METRO are primarily domestic strategic consolidation cases. They should not be misclassified as foreign PE control.

**PLAUSIBLE HYPOTHESIS:** Institutional capital may affect price-pack architecture, channel incentives, SKU prioritization, private labels, delivery fees, advertising auctions, and quick-commerce assortment. This is plausible but not proven for India in the current evidence set.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** International food-system evidence shows why the India research design must be product- and channel-specific. NBER consumer-product evidence finds that PE targets increased revenue and product offerings; average product-line prices rose, but same existing product/store price effects were much smaller, pointing to mix and geography [S83]. Chevalier's supermarket LBO evidence shows pricing effects vary with local competition and rival leverage [S85]. Bernstein and Sheen's restaurant-chain evidence reports improved health inspections and lower menu prices after PE buyouts, alongside lower headcount [S86]. These findings argue against one-directional assumptions.

### 6.3 Agriculture Inputs and Seeds

**VERIFIED FACT:** Advanta is a global seeds platform with foreign institutional investment. UPL disclosed Alpha Wave's US$350 million investment for approximately 12.5% and noted a prior US$300 million KKR investment in October 2022 [S26].

**PLAUSIBLE HYPOTHESIS:** Foreign institutional capital in seed platforms can influence R&D priorities, acquisition strategy, and commercialization. UPL's release explicitly references organic and inorganic growth and a potential IPO path [S26].

**UNPROVEN CLAIM:** Farmer harm from Advanta's PE-backed capital structure is not established. Required evidence would include crop-level seed pricing, farmer switching costs, licensing terms, germplasm access, yield performance, and market-share changes.

**VERIFIED FACT:** Official rural/farmer baseline data are now in the evidence base. NABARD's NAFIS 2021-22 press release reports that average monthly rural household income rose from Rs 8,059 in 2016-17 to Rs 12,698 in 2021-22, expenditure rose from Rs 6,646 to Rs 11,262, food share in consumption declined from 51% to 47%, households with outstanding debt rose from 47.4% to 52.0%, agricultural households relying only on institutional loans rose from 60.5% to 75.5%, and average landholding fell from 1.08 ha to 0.74 ha [S142]. MoSPI/NSO's NSS 77th Round report estimates 93.094 million agricultural households in 2018-19 and average monthly agricultural-household income of Rs 10,218, including wages Rs 4,063, crop production Rs 3,798, farming of animals Rs 1,582, non-farm business Rs 641 and leasing out land Rs 134 [S143].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Farmer bargaining-power analysis must be crop- and channel-specific. MoSPI/NSO reports 50.2% indebted agricultural households, average outstanding loan of Rs 74,121 and 69.6% of outstanding loans from institutional sources [S143]. Its crop-sale tables show that sale channels vary by crop: local markets/local traders are usually dominant for listed crops, while APMC, government, cooperative, private processor and contract-sponsor roles differ sharply, with sugarcane showing unusually high private processor/contract-sponsor shares [S143]. These facts define the baseline and confounders; they do not show PE-caused farmer harm.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** International food-chain evidence identifies farmer-impact mechanisms to test. OECD cautions that margin and pass-through claims require chain-specific data [S87]. A Safeway-Albertsons specialty-crop study reports grower concerns and evidence consistent with lower prices received, lost revenue, higher transaction costs and volatility in relevant settings [S88]. This supports a farmer-impact research agenda for India but not a finding of PE-caused farmer harm.

### 6.4 Nutrition

**UNPROVEN CLAIM:** The repository does not prove that PE/institutional capital worsens nutrition outcomes in India. Haldiram is a packaged-food manufacturer and seller of snacks, sweets, RTE, dairy, bakery, chocolates, and non-carbonated RTD beverages [S17-S19], but nutritional impact requires product-level reformulation, consumption, labeling, and health-outcome evidence.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Nutrition risk is real even if PE causality is unproven. ICMR-NIN's 2024 dietary guidance estimates that 56.4% of India's total disease burden is due to unhealthy diets and recommends minimizing HFSS and UPF consumption [S53]. WHO India's UPF report analyses five UPF categories and reports 13.37% CAGR in UPF retail-sales value from 2011 to 2021; salty snacks grew faster at 16.78% CAGR and increased from 14% to 19% of UPF retail-sales value over 2011-2021 [S54]. WHO also identifies nutrition labelling and front-of-pack labelling as tools for healthier choices and reformulation [S55], while FAO provides the broader UPF/diet-quality framework [S56].

**EXPERT INTERPRETATION:** For food-system monitoring, the right question is not only "who owns the company" but whether institutional capital changes product mix, pack sizes, marketing, discounting, channel placement, private labels, and formulation. The current evidence verifies ownership and platformization; it does not measure nutrition outcomes.

## 7. Policy and Regulatory Analysis

### 7.1 FDI Policy

**VERIFIED FACT:** India's FDI regime is permissive in the relevant sectors, but not uniform. Manufacturing is 100% automatic [S01]. Food-product retail trading for food manufactured/produced in India is 100% government approval route [S01, S04]. Pharma greenfield is 100% automatic; pharma brownfield is 74% automatic and government route beyond 74% [S01]. Medical-device manufacturing is 100% automatic [S01].

**VERIFIED FACT:** The historical policy baseline is now primary-sourced. The 1991 Statement on Industrial Policy supports the macro liberalization narrative but not a sector-specific PE-causation claim [S106]. DPIIT's 2010 consolidated FDI policy stated that unlisted sectors were permitted up to 100% FDI on the automatic route, listed construction-development projects including hospitals at 100% automatic, exempted hospitals from selected construction conditionalities, and kept retail trading generally prohibited except single-brand retail [S105].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Healthcare investment policy is explicitly capital-attracting. NITI Aayog states that FDI is permitted up to 100% automatic in the hospital sector and medical devices, and its report frames India's healthcare industry as "ripe for investment" [S05].

**VERIFIED FACT:** CCI's combination framework is based on statutory thresholds and exemptions, not a healthcare-specific public-interest test. CCI's public filing guidance describes combination assessment under Section 5 and notes the March 2024 de-minimis exemption for targets with India assets not more than INR 450 crore or turnover not more than INR 1,250 crore for two years [S43].

**VERIFIED FACT:** FSSAI's current labelling framework regulates pre-packaged food labelling and display, including nutrition declarations [S42]. This is nutrition-governance context, but it is not evidence that packaged-food institutional ownership improves or worsens nutrition outcomes.

### 7.2 Health Policy

**VERIFIED FACT:** NHP 2017's goals include reducing out-of-pocket expenditure, reducing catastrophic expenditure, aligning private-sector growth with public-health goals, and raising government health expenditure to 2.5% of GDP by 2025 [S06, S07].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** India's health-financing system creates serious financial-protection risk, but the repository's "8-9% of households in 2023" wording should be corrected. NITI Aayog, citing Brookings/NSSO analysis, reports that over 7% of India's population is pushed into poverty every year due to healthcare costs and shows impoverishing health expenditure of 7.5% in 2004 and 8.5% in 2014. The World Bank's societal-poverty-line OOPE indicator has no 2023 India value in the captured API, with the latest non-null value at 5.6% in 2011 [S125, S126].

**EXPERT INTERPRETATION:** The policy architecture is internally conflicted. It seeks private-sector engagement and strategic purchasing, while also requiring stronger regulation and affordability. That makes PE investment neither inherently illegitimate nor automatically public-interest aligned.

**VERIFIED FACT:** AB-PMJAY is a large public-payer channel. Official sources report 12 crore families covered, about 42.48 crore Ayushman cards created, 10.98 crore authorized admissions worth Rs 1.60 lakh crore, and 32,574 empanelled hospitals including 15,532 private hospitals as of 1 December 2025 [S75]. By 31 December 2025, more than 15,733 private hospitals were empanelled [S74].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** PM-JAY creates both an access channel and a policy incentive for private hospitals, but private participation is uneven and date-sensitive. Peer-reviewed literature supports an older rough 20% frame for private-hospital participation and a Maharashtra state-case finding of only 13% private-hospital empanelment, with few large hospitals participating [S137, S138]. MoHFW states that NHA revised the Health Benefit Package, increased rates for 350 packages, launched HEM 2.0, monitored claim settlement, and set up a hospital-specific call center to improve private-hospital participation [S78]. BMC's Maharashtra study identifies low reimbursement rates, fixed packages, delayed reimbursements, claims complexity and economic non-viability as barriers [S137]. Chain-level data are still needed to test whether PE-backed or premium hospitals participate meaningfully and whether patients face denials, balance billing, or limited package availability.

**VERIFIED FACT:** The Clinical Establishments Act is unevenly adopted rather than simply absent. MoHFW's portal states that the central Act has been adopted in 19 States/UTs [S76]. Its proactive disclosure describes public/private registration, display of charges and facilities, public data, emergency-treatment obligations, and state implementation responsibilities [S77]. Enforcement quality remains an open empirical question.

### 7.3 Competition Policy

**VERIFIED FACT:** CCI reviews many of these combinations. CCI approved Aster-QCIL, KKR-HCG, Temasek-Haldiram, Alpha Wave-Haldiram, L Catterton-Haldiram, and Torrent-JB [S10, S13, S15, S17-S19].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** CCI's pharma review can identify product-level risks and require remedies, as Torrent-JB shows [S15].

**PLAUSIBLE HYPOTHESIS:** CCI's standard competition mandate may not cover all patient-welfare risks in hospital consolidation, such as medical debt, public-insurance participation, value extraction, staffing, and clinical outcomes. This is a policy gap, not proof of regulatory failure.

### 7.4 Tax and Investment Incentives

**VERIFIED FACT:** Food-processing policy includes large-scale public incentives. MoFPI states that PLISFPI has a Rs 10,900 crore outlay, aims to create global food manufacturing champions, supports eligible food-product segments and branding/marketing abroad, and runs for 2021-22 to 2026-27 [S90]. PIB/MoFPI reports 168 approved applicants, about Rs 9,207 crore cumulative investment, about 35 LMT annual processing/preservation capacity and Rs 2,714.79 crore incentives disbursed as of 31 December 2025 [S89].

**VERIFIED FACT:** PMKSY and related post-harvest programs support private-sector food-processing and supply-chain infrastructure. PIB/MoFPI reports FY 2024-25 PMKSY allocation of Rs 630 crore and grant eligibility for public and private companies, firms, partnerships, LLPs, cooperatives, FPOs/FPCs and SHGs [S91]. A separate PIB/MoFPI release reports 1,601 approved PMKSY projects, 1,133 operational projects, Rs 5,365.32 crore approved grant, 255.66 LMT preservation/processing capacity, 34,15,102 farmers benefited and 4,33,639 employment generated as of 30 June 2025 [S92].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Tax policy can improve the economics of infrastructure-heavy assets relevant to this investigation. Income Tax Department guidance lists cold-chain facilities, agri-produce warehousing and hospitals with at least 100 beds among Section 35AD specified businesses [S93].

**VERIFIED FACT:** India's fund-policy architecture is now partly mapped. SEBI's 2012 AIF release states that AIFs including private equity funds must register with SEBI and that Category II AIFs include private equity funds, debt funds and funds of funds, with no specific incentives or concessions and restricted leverage [S113]. IFSCA's 2025 Fund Management Regulations state that fund managers cannot operate in IFSC without IFSCA FME registration and create Authorised FME, Registered FME Non-Retail and Registered FME Retail categories; non-retail FMEs can launch restricted schemes by private placement, including Category II AIF schemes [S114]. Income Tax Department section 47 text and IFSCA's brochure establish an IFSC/offshore-fund relocation framework under specified conditions; the IFSCA brochure reports 23 relocated schemes, US$9.14 billion cumulative commitments, US$3.56 billion funds raised and US$4.38 billion cumulative investments as of September 30, 2025 [S115, S116].

**EXPERT INTERPRETATION:** The ownership-transparency concern is real but should be stated carefully. Reviewed filings and orders verify repeated use of foreign holding and investment vehicles, including Singapore, Mauritius and Netherlands entities in healthcare, food and platform transactions [S13, S18, S19, S70, S72, S107, S117]. Those structures can make ultimate beneficial ownership and governance rights harder to interpret from public documents. The current evidence does not prove unlawful evasion, concealment intent or treaty abuse in any specific transaction.

**EXPERT INTERPRETATION:** These incentives and fund rules are best understood as investment-enabling conditions, not proof of PE-specific favoritism. They can make food-processing, cold-chain, warehousing, hospital and fund-management projects more attractive to institutional capital, but beneficiary-level ownership, deal-level fund routing, treaty application, side letters and lobbying evidence remain unresolved.

## 8. Influence, Lobbying, and Governance

**VERIFIED FACT:** Advisory and governance channels can exist even without majority ownership. L Catterton's Haldiram order identifies an advisory agreement involving commercially sensitive information and advice on strategy, talent, and performance [S19].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Minority institutional investors can have influence through board seats, reserved matters, veto rights, information rights, advisory agreements, and financing terms. However, most such rights are not public in the current source set.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** The Bhupendra Singh/NPPA transfer episode supports governance-risk scrutiny but not a proven capture finding. PIB verifies that the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet moved Singh from NPPA chairperson to chairperson of the National Authority for Chemical Weapons Convention on March 1, 2018 [S128]. IndiaSpend verifies that pro-access activists alleged industry and hospital-lobby pressure after NPPA pricing and hospital-margin actions, but the reviewed record does not prove motive or causation [S129].

**UNPROVEN CLAIM:** Regulatory capture is not proven by the evidence reviewed. The repository cites circumstantial indicators and strong language, but a forensic capture finding would require lobbying submissions, consultation records, revolving-door timelines, internal correspondence, enforcement deviation analysis, and documented quid-pro-quo evidence.

## 9. Impact Assessment

### 9.1 Healthcare Affordability

**VERIFIED FACT:** India's official OOPE remains high at 43.4% of total health expenditure in 2022-23, although below the repository's current 54.8% claim [S08].

**VERIFIED FACT:** The repository's public-private hospital expenditure ratio is broadly correct when framed as NSS 2017-18 hospitalization expenditure excluding childbirth. MoSPI reports average private-hospital medical expenditure per hospitalization case of Rs 27,347 rural and Rs 38,822 urban versus government/public Rs 4,290 and Rs 4,837, equal to about 6.37x rural and 8.03x urban; the combined all-India private/public ratio was about 7.15x [S127]. This does not prove PE ownership caused the gap.

**VERIFIED FACT:** NPPA's archived Fortis Memorial Research Institute Gurgaon tables show extreme billing-rate/procurement margins in the Adya Singh dengue case: up to 343% for scheduled formulations, 914% for non-scheduled formulations and 1,737% for consumables, including the 1,737% three-way stop-cock example [S31].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Insurance claims and grievances are material. IRDAI reports that in FY 2024-25 insurers settled about 87% of registered health-claim counts, repudiated about 8%, and had about 5% pending as of 31 March 2025 [S79]. It also reports 2,57,790 Bima Bharosa grievances in FY 2024-25, including 1,37,361 general/health grievances; 69% of general-insurance grievances were claims-related [S79].

**VERIFIED FACT:** NITI Aayog's investment report describes structural demand pressure: India had 1.3 hospital beds per 1,000 population, needed an additional 3 million beds to reach 3 per 1,000 by 2025, and faced uneven bed distribution across states [S05]. These indicators explain why capital targets hospitals; they do not prove that PE ownership caused higher prices.

**PLAUSIBLE HYPOTHESIS:** PE-backed hospitals may emphasize high-ARPOB services, payer mix, specialty care, procurement economies, and margin expansion. This follows from investor incentives and international PE-healthcare literature [S27, S28], but India-specific causal estimates are missing.

### 9.2 Healthcare Accessibility

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Foreign-backed capital can expand capacity. Aster-QCIL, Manipal, HCG, and Baby Memorial all present expansion narratives; Manipal's 2026 SEBI abridged prospectus gives a current example with 48 hospitals and 12,367 licensed beds as of September 30, 2025 [S10-S14, S21, S24, S117].

**PLAUSIBLE HYPOTHESIS:** Expansion may skew toward urban, Tier 1-3, tertiary, and insured/cash-paying markets rather than low-margin rural/primary care. NITI Aayog itself emphasizes infrastructure gaps and private-sector expansion beyond metros [S05], but chain-level service distribution needs data.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** The investment thesis is partly a capacity-gap thesis. NITI Aayog states that hospital chains are expanding into Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities and that non-metros offer lower real-estate costs and rising demand [S05]. Whether that expansion improves access for low-income patients depends on prices, payer acceptance, emergency-care policies, and PM-JAY participation.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** PM-JAY has substantial private-hospital empanelment by late-2025 official counts, including more than 15,733 private hospitals as of 31 December 2025 and 1,259 private hospitals in aspirational districts [S74]. But peer-reviewed studies show why aggregate counts can overstate effective access: PLOS One found uneven distribution and only one private empanelled hospital per 100,000 eligible population nationally in March 2020, while BMC found only 13% private-hospital empanelment in Maharashtra and limited large-hospital participation [S137, S138]. This is evidence of a public-payer access channel through private hospitals. It is not evidence that every major corporate or PE-backed chain participates at meaningful volume.

### 9.3 Quality

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** International evidence warns of quality risks. BMJ and JAMA findings justify monitoring quality, adverse events, staffing, infection rates, falls, readmissions, and mortality after PE acquisitions [S27, S28].

**UNPROVEN CLAIM:** No India-wide causal evidence reviewed here proves PE acquisition reduces clinical quality in Indian hospitals.

### 9.4 Workforce

**PLAUSIBLE HYPOTHESIS:** PE/platform ownership can alter staffing ratios, compensation structures, productivity targets, procurement protocols, and clinical autonomy. This is plausible from international evidence and financialisation literature [S27-S29], but not quantified for India in this package.

### 9.5 Food System

**VERIFIED FACT:** Institutional capital is entering packaged foods and seeds/agriculture inputs [S17-S19, S25, S26].

**VERIFIED FACT:** Rural and farmer baseline data are available from official sources. NABARD NAFIS and MoSPI/NSO NSS 77th Round provide income, expenditure, food-share, debt, landholding, agricultural-household income composition, indebtedness and crop-sale-channel data [S142, S143].

**UNPROVEN CLAIM:** Consumer price increases, nutrition deterioration, farmer-income harm, or food-security deterioration caused by PE/institutional ownership are not proven. Required data include category-level prices, SKU composition, sodium/sugar/fat reformulation, market shares, distributor margins, farmgate prices, contract terms, procurement concentration, crop-wise sale-channel exposure and ownership-change event studies.

**OPEN EVIDENCE GAP:** A concentration screen has been added, but no robust HHI, CR4, or city/category concentration table has been computed. Transaction evidence is not a substitute for market-share analysis. The screen also shows why repository claims about PE control of organized beds should remain unverified until the organized-bed denominator is built.

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Public-health nutrition sources justify monitoring packaged-food and quick-commerce growth for UPF/HFSS exposure [S53-S56]. The monitoring should be transaction-linked, not allegation-driven: for Haldiram, Balaji, Theobroma, Capital Foods, Instamart and Blinkit, the relevant tests are SKU nutrient profiles, reformulation, pack size, price, marketing and channel placement before and after ownership or capital events.

## 10. International Comparative Evidence

### United States

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** US evidence shows material risks. JAMA found increased hospital-acquired adverse events after PE acquisition in a Medicare hospital sample [S28]. BMJ found PE ownership often associated with higher costs and mixed-to-worse quality across healthcare studies [S27].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** US nursing-home evidence identifies mechanisms that should be monitored in India if similar ownership structures emerge: NBER's digest of Gupta et al. reports higher mortality, fewer caregiver hours, higher charges, and large increases in interest and lease payments after PE acquisitions [S49].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** The US policy response now provides concrete regulatory comparators. Oregon SB 951 was enrolled as Chapter 295 and restricts ownership/control conduct involving professional medical entities and management-services organizations [S95]. California AB 1415 expands the state health-care market oversight framework, including market monitoring of venture capital activity, profit margins, consolidation, competition, prices, access, quality and equity [S96]. Maine section 1730-A temporarily bars PE companies and REITs from acquiring or increasing direct/indirect ownership interest or operational/financial control in hospitals until its repeal date [S97]. The repository's "at least 15 states" claim is directionally supported by DLA Piper, but the denominator is definition-dependent; NCSL uses a broader notice-law frame and reports at least 35 states requiring notice for certain healthcare mergers, closures or affiliations [S98, S99].

Lesson for India: require ownership disclosure, related-party transaction reporting, debt/value extraction monitoring, MSO/management-control review, and quality metrics after acquisitions.

### United Kingdom

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** The Hinchingbrooke/Circle case is a cautionary private-management comparator, not a direct PE-ownership analogue. The NAO found that the first private company to manage an NHS hospital had made some early clinical improvements but faced major financial challenges [S65]. CQC later recorded serious quality concerns while Circle Partnership had managed the trust, and noted that Circle had ceased involvement by the time of the follow-up release [S64].

Lesson for India: PPP and private-management contracts need enforceable quality, financial, and step-in protections. Contracting out management does not remove public accountability for quality and continuity of care.

### Sweden and Europe

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Swedish residential-care evidence suggests that ownership form matters within non-public providers. A panel study of 2,639 facilities found that PE-owned and publicly traded operators often ended up lower in the service-quality distribution than private limited companies and nonprofits [S50].

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** WHO/European Observatory's long-term care review frames PE as an intensified version of for-profit ownership risk and identifies policy options including acquisition disclosure and approval, ownership transparency, and regulation across the PE lifecycle [S52].

Lesson for India: do not treat all "private" or "non-government" providers as equivalent. Governance rights, debt, real-estate leases, staffing, public reimbursement, and quality indicators should be visible to regulators.

### Australia

**VERIFIED FACT:** Australian evidence shows rising PE acquisitions in healthcare delivery assets from 2008 to 2022, especially general practice, imaging, and ophthalmology, while warning that local impact evidence remains underdeveloped [S51].

Lesson for India: diagnostics and imaging roll-ups should be monitored early, before market structure becomes opaque and hard to unwind.

### Canada

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Canadian long-term-care evidence strengthens the warning on financialized ownership, staffing, real-estate structures, and vulnerable patients. Statistics Canada summarizes research associating for-profit ownership with increased COVID outbreaks and resident deaths in Canadian LTC, while emphasizing design standards, chain affiliation, and staffing as mechanisms [S67]. Waterloo's research summary reports substantial financialized ownership in seniors housing and LTC and highlights mortality/quality concerns [S68].

Lesson for India: if elder care, hospital real estate, or long-stay care becomes an institutional-capital target, regulators should monitor staffing, leaseback structures, ownership transparency, and resident outcomes.

### Brazil

**EXPERT INTERPRETATION:** Brazil's Rede D'Or example is an emerging-market capacity-expansion analogue. Carlyle's 2015 investment supported growth at a large private hospital operator with more than 4,500 beds and a stated plan for major facility investment [S66]. This demonstrates the potential benefit side of institutional capital: expansion and platform building.

Lesson for India: capacity expansion is not enough by itself. Large hospital platforms should be paired with concentration, pricing, payer-mix, and quality monitoring.

### Chile

**VERIFIED FACT:** Chile's FNE blocked Nexus Chile's proposed acquisition of Isapre Colmena after concluding that the transaction would substantially lessen competition in private health insurance and could increase plan prices [S69].

Lesson for India: health-financing and TPA markets may need sector-specific merger scrutiny where payer concentration can affect premiums, provider bargaining, and patient access.

### DFI and Other Markets

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Oxfam and broader DFI literature identify risks when public/development finance supports for-profit hospital chains without strong safeguards [S30]. These are advocacy sources and require careful verification, but they identify plausible access and affordability risks relevant to India.

Lesson for India: development finance and public insurance should be conditioned on measurable access, price, and quality commitments.

### Food-System Comparators

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Food-system comparator evidence is mixed. PE-backed packaged consumer-product firms can grow through product launches, expansion into richer areas, and product-mix changes rather than only direct same-product price increases [S83, S84]. Supermarket LBO price effects depend on rival leverage and local competition [S85]. Restaurant-chain buyouts can show operational improvements and lower prices while reducing headcount [S86]. Retail grocery mergers can create upstream producer risks through buyer power, uncertainty, transaction costs and price volatility [S88].

Lesson for India: food-system claims need SKU-level prices, pack-size data, nutrition panels, city/channel shares, discounting, delivery fees, procurement contracts, farmgate prices, volatility and worker data. Ownership changes alone are not enough.

### Transferability Warning

International evidence should not be pasted onto India without adjustment. India's payer mix, OOPE burden, public-system capacity, price controls, CCI structure, and state-level healthcare regulation differ from the US and UK. International evidence is a risk signal, not a substitute for Indian data.

## 11. Causal Analysis

### Why Investments Occurred

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Investment occurred because of demand growth, under-bedded healthcare markets, rising incomes, insurance growth, medical tourism, fragmented providers, high expected returns, permissive FDI, and public policy support for capacity-building in food processing and supply chains. NITI Aayog's healthcare report explicitly frames these segments as investment opportunities [S05]. EY and EY-IVCA show continued PE/VC interest in life sciences, healthcare buyouts, hospitals/clinics and healthcare-tech assets [S09, S140]. Grant Thornton/AHPI and Bain-IVCA reinforce the same incentive picture through large healthcare/pharma M&A plus PE deal aggregates, hospital-heavy deal value, and 2024 healthcare deal-volume growth [S139, S141]. MoFPI incentive data shows policy-backed scale and cold-chain investment in food processing [S89-S92].

### Which Policy Changes Mattered

**STRONG EVIDENCE-BASED CONCLUSION:** Liberal FDI routes mattered by allowing foreign capital to enter, often without prior government approval. Food-processing PLI, PMKSY/cold-chain grants, Section 35AD specified-business treatment, SEBI's domestic AIF framework, and IFSCA/Income Tax IFSC fund-management and relocation rules also matter as investment-enabling policies [S89-S93, S113-S116]. But policy alone did not cause each transaction. Deals also depended on promoter willingness to sell, public-market exit paths, chain scalability, public-payer and insurance growth, and sponsor fundraising cycles.

### Did Consolidation Alter Market Behavior?

**PLAUSIBLE HYPOTHESIS:** In healthcare, consolidation may raise bargaining power with suppliers and insurers, concentrate specialty services, and change pricing. Torrent-JB shows CCI concern in pharma FDF markets [S15]. Hospital-sector concentration effects require city-level catchment analysis and payer-segment data.

### PE Ownership or Broader Industry Trends?

Many observed outcomes are not uniquely PE-driven. Corporate hospital overcharging, branded-drug markups, diagnostics incentives, medical inflation, and public-sector undercapacity predate many PE transactions. PE can amplify those incentives, but proving amplification requires before/after ownership analysis and matched controls.

The detailed causal assessment is recorded in `impact_causality_matrix.csv`. Its core conclusion is that policy enablement, transaction activity, insurance/TPA claims-flow relevance, and international risk/mechanism signals are well supported, while India-specific causal claims about prices, clinical outcomes, farmer harm, nutrition deterioration, and regulatory capture remain unproven without outcome datasets.

## 12. Scenarios, 2026-2041

### Best Case

**Likelihood: 25-35%.** Foreign capital expands beds, diagnostics, devices, oncology, fertility, pharma manufacturing, and food distribution while India adds ownership transparency, healthcare-specific transaction review, public-insurance participation conditions, patient-price monitoring, and stronger public health spending. Benefits include capacity expansion, better procurement, technology upgrades, professional management, and faster diffusion of specialty care.

### Base Case

**Likelihood: 45-55%.** Consolidation continues in hospitals, oncology, medical devices, pharma platforms, packaged foods, and agriculture inputs. Regulation remains mostly competition-law based, with incremental disclosure. Access expands in profitable urban and Tier 2-3 markets, while affordability pressures persist for uninsured households. Food-system effects remain mixed and hard to detect.

### Worst Case

**Likelihood: 15-25%.** Roll-ups reduce local competition in specialty care, pricing power rises, payer mix and high-margin services dominate, public-insurance participation stays weak, and value extraction through debt, management fees, real-estate structures, and dividend recapitalizations becomes significant. In food, institutional ownership and quick-commerce dynamics increase consumer prices or reduce product quality without farmer gains. This is plausible but not currently proven in India.

## 13. Recommendations

1. Create healthcare-specific transaction review for hospital, diagnostics, oncology, fertility, dialysis, and emergency-care platforms above defined thresholds.
2. Require ultimate beneficial ownership disclosure for hospitals, diagnostics chains, and public-insurance empanelled providers.
3. Require chain-level and facility-level disclosure of bed count, specialty mix, ARPOB, payer mix, PMJAY participation, claim-denial rates, quality indicators, and adverse-event indicators.
4. Require related-party transaction, lease, debt, management-fee, dividend, and recapitalization disclosure for healthcare providers above size thresholds.
5. Expand CCI healthcare analysis to include local catchment areas, specialty-service concentration, insurer bargaining power, and patient switching costs.
6. Establish a national hospital pricing and billing transparency dataset, including consumables and diagnostics.
7. Condition DFI/public financing and public-insurance empanelment on access, emergency-care, price, and quality commitments.
8. For packaged foods, track category-level concentration, price-pack changes, sodium/sugar/fat reformulation, marketing to children, and quick-commerce channel dependence.
9. For seeds/agriculture inputs, monitor market share, seed pricing, contract terms, germplasm access, crop outcomes, and farmer switching costs.
10. Correct public debate by separating verified ownership facts from unproven claims of control, corruption, or causation.

The major findings classification in section 14 and the recommendations in section 13 do not change as a result of the additions; the body of verified evidence supporting them has expanded.

Known limitations remain: private MCA filings, shareholder agreements, fund LP records and side letters were not retrieved; full product/city-level HHI/CR4 tables were not buildable from public evidence, although hospital-bed proxies and insurance/TPA concentration proxies were added; hospital-level pricing and quality panels were not built; India-specific SKU food-price and farmer price-transmission panels were not available in the repository; deal-level fund routing/treaty/GIFT-IFSC structuring was not completed; and insurance/TPA impact causality and diagnostic-chain market-share mapping remain evidence-limited.
