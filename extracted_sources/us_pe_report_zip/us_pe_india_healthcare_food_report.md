US Private Equity firms have invested **over $8 billion** in Indian healthcare and pharmaceutical assets between 2020 and 2026, with **Blackstone, KKR, Carlyle, TPG, and Advent International** leading an unprecedented acquisition spree of hospital chains, cancer care networks, and drug manufacturers. In the food sector, PE-backed transactions — including Haldiram's at a **$10 billion valuation** — signal deepening foreign control over consumer staples. While proponents argue these investments address India's acute healthcare infrastructure deficit (only **1.3 hospital beds per 1,000 people** versus the OECD average of 4.3), mounting evidence from academic research, patient advocacy reports, and investigative journalism reveals a darker pattern: **rising medical costs** (private hospitals now charge **6.4–8.0x** more than public facilities), allegations of **profit-driven medical malpractices**, and a structural shift toward corporatised care that excludes India's poorest. The question is no longer whether US PE firms are taking over India's health and food sectors — the data confirms they are — but whether this takeover serves the Indian public interest or primarily enriches global fund managers and their limited partners.

# US Private Equity's Deepening Grip on India's Healthcare, Pharmaceutical, and Food Sectors: A Comprehensive Investigation (2020–2026)

**Report Date:** June 3, 2026

## 1. Executive Summary: The Scale of US Private Equity Penetration

The period from 2020 to 2026 has witnessed an unprecedented influx of US private equity (PE) capital into India's foundational economic sectors — healthcare, pharmaceuticals, and food processing. This report, based on an exhaustive review of financial disclosures, regulatory filings, academic research, investigative reports, and news archives, documents a systematic and accelerating acquisition strategy by the world's largest alternative asset managers. The evidence reveals not merely passive portfolio investments, but active efforts to secure **controlling stakes** in critical infrastructure that affects the daily lives of 1.4 billion Indians. The implications extend beyond financial markets into questions of national sovereignty over essential services, patient welfare, food security, and the structural direction of India's economic development.

### 1.1 Key Findings at a Glance

The investigation reveals that US-based PE firms have deployed **billions of dollars** across Indian healthcare and food assets, fundamentally restructuring ownership patterns in sectors traditionally dominated by family-owned businesses and public institutions. In healthcare alone, PE and venture capital inflows exceeded **$15.5 billion** over the last five years, with the hospital segment attracting **$4.96 billion in PE investments** and **$6.74 billion in M&A deals** between 2022 and 2024  [(Dealflow IQ)](https://www.dealflowiq.com/p/the-india-hospitals-report-2025-capital) . The pharmaceutical sector has seen equally transformative transactions, with Advent International's exit from Bharat Serums and Vaccines at **$1.63 billion** and KKR's sale of JB Pharma to Torrent Pharmaceuticals at **$3.1 billion** representing two of the largest deals in recent Indian pharma history  [(EY)](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-in/insights/private-equity/documents/ey-private-equity-and-venture-capital-trendbook-2025-v1.pdf) . In food processing, the **$10 billion valuation** of Haldiram's Snacks Food — with investments from Temasek, Alpha Wave Global, and L Catterton — signals that PE interest has penetrated even India's most iconic consumer brands  [(The Economic Times)](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/temasek-signs-agreement-to-buy-10-in-haldiram-snacks-food/articleshow/118946807.cms) .

### 1.2 Methodology and Source Coverage

This investigation employed a multi-source research methodology encompassing **over 12 rounds of targeted web searches** across financial databases, regulatory filings, academic repositories, investigative journalism archives, and civil society reports. Primary sources include Bain & Company's annual India Private Equity Report, EY's Private Equity and Venture Capital Trendbook, Becker's Hospital Review, the University of Pennsylvania's Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI), Oxfam International's investigative reports, and official filings with the Competition Commission of India (CCI) and Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI). The analysis covers the period from January 2020 to June 2026, with particular attention to transactions exceeding $100 million and controlling-stake acquisitions. Every factual claim in this report is traceable to publicly available documentation, with citation markers linking to source materials.

### 1.3 Scope and Limitations

The investigation focuses specifically on **US-headquartered private equity firms** and their investments in Indian healthcare (hospitals, diagnostics, medical devices), pharmaceuticals (APIs, formulations, CDMOs), and food processing (packaged foods, agriculture, FMCG). While non-US firms (Singapore's Temasek, UAE's Alpha Wave, Sweden's EQT) also feature prominently in the ecosystem, they are discussed primarily as co-investors or competitors to US PE firms. The report does not cover venture capital investments in early-stage healthtech startups, nor does it extensively treat real estate or infrastructure PE deals unless directly related to hospital assets. A key limitation is the opacity of private equity transactions — many deal values are undisclosed, and limited partners' identities in PE funds are often confidential. Additionally, the full social impact of PE ownership on patient outcomes, drug pricing, and food affordability requires longitudinal study that extends beyond the timeframe of this investigation.

## 2. The Healthcare Takeover: US PE Firms and Indian Hospitals

India's hospital sector has emerged as the **single most attractive destination** for US private equity capital in the country's healthcare ecosystem. Between 2022 and 2024, the hospital segment alone accounted for **$15.3 billion** in deal value — representing over 50% of total healthcare sector transactions  [(Mondaq)](https://www.mondaq.com/india/healthcare/1629796/code-of-care-an-overview-of-india%60s-key-healthcare-laws) . This concentration reflects a deliberate strategy by US PE firms to acquire controlling stakes in hospital chains, specialty care networks, and diagnostic platforms, leveraging India's 100% FDI automatic route for hospital investments  [(Lexology)](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=43f53311-5aaf-42f7-b869-ec86ed9a0b38) . The following sections document the major transactions, the firms behind them, and the accumulating evidence of adverse consequences for patients and healthcare workers.

### 2.1 Blackstone's Billion-Dollar Hospital Empire

Blackstone Inc., the world's largest alternative asset manager with over **$1.3 trillion in AUM**  [(Praxis Rock)](https://praxisrock.com/insights/top-private-equity-firms) , made its decisive entry into Indian healthcare in October 2023 through a pair of transactions that created one of the country's largest hospital platforms. The firm's strategy was not to build hospitals organically, but to acquire existing chains and consolidate them under a unified ownership structure — a classic PE playbook of roll-up acquisitions followed by operational optimisation and eventual exit.

#### 2.1.1 The Care Hospitals Acquisition ($800 Million)

In October 2023, Blackstone announced the acquisition of approximately **73% of Quality Care India Ltd (QCIL)**, the holding company that owns the CARE Hospitals chain, from TPG's Evercare platform for approximately **$800 million** (₹6,640 crore)  [(mergersindia.com)](https://mnacritique.mergersindia.com/news/blackstone-to-buy-controlling-stakes-in-care-hospitals-kims-health-for-1-bn/) . The transaction, which received approval from the Competition Commission of India (CCI) in September 2023, transferred control of a 16-hospital network spanning 16 cities with approximately 2,400 beds  [(AVCJ)](https://www.avcj.com/avcj/news/3030016/indian-regulator-approves-blackstones-purchase-of-care-hospitals) . TPG's Rise Funds retained approximately 25% as a minority stakeholder, ensuring continued involvement by the previous owner  [(mergersindia.com)](https://mnacritique.mergersindia.com/news/blackstone-to-buy-controlling-stakes-in-care-hospitals-kims-health-for-1-bn/) . The deal was not without controversy — rival hospital operator Max Healthcare Institute moved the Bombay High Court in April 2023, alleging breach of an exclusivity agreement, but the court-appointed arbitrator rejected Max's claim in July 2023, clearing the path for Blackstone  [(AVCJ)](https://www.avcj.com/avcj/news/3030016/indian-regulator-approves-blackstones-purchase-of-care-hospitals) . This legal battle underscored the intense competition among PE firms and strategic buyers for control of Indian hospital assets.

#### 2.1.2 KIMS Health Integration and the Combined Platform

Simultaneously with the CARE acquisition, Blackstone and TPG jointly used QCIL as a vehicle to sign a definitive agreement for the acquisition of an **80% stake in Kerala Institute of Medical Sciences (KIMS Health)**, buying out the entire 61% stake held by Indian private equity firm True North  [(mergersindia.com)](https://mnacritique.mergersindia.com/news/blackstone-to-buy-controlling-stakes-in-care-hospitals-kims-health-for-1-bn/) . Blackstone invested approximately **$300 million** (₹2,490 crore) in KIMS, while TPG contributed approximately $100 million (₹830 crore)  [(mergersindia.com)](https://mnacritique.mergersindia.com/news/blackstone-to-buy-controlling-stakes-in-care-hospitals-kims-health-for-1-bn/) . KIMS founder Dr. M. I. Sahadulla retained approximately 20%, continuing in a leadership role  [(mergersindia.com)](https://mnacritique.mergersindia.com/news/blackstone-to-buy-controlling-stakes-in-care-hospitals-kims-health-for-1-bn/) . The combined platform of CARE Hospitals and KIMS Health created a network of **23 hospitals and 4,000 beds across 16 cities**, positioning Blackstone as the operator of India's fourth-largest hospital group  [(Becker's Hospital Review)](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/hospital-transactions-and-valuation/blackstone-to-acquire-16-hospital-system-in-india/) . Blackstone's total commitment across both transactions exceeded **$1 billion** (₹8,300 crore), with the enterprise valuation of the combined platform reaching approximately **₹11,000 crore** including debt  [(mergersindia.com)](https://mnacritique.mergersindia.com/news/blackstone-to-buy-controlling-stakes-in-care-hospitals-kims-health-for-1-bn/) .

#### 2.1.3 Strategic Expansion Plans and Bed Capacity Targets

Since acquiring CARE Hospitals and KIMS Health, Blackstone has pursued an aggressive expansion strategy. In 2025, the firm was reported to be among the bidders for Sahyadri Hospitals, Maharashtra's largest private hospital network, with industry estimates valuing the chain at approximately **$800 million**  [(Countercurrents)](https://countercurrents.org/2026/05/between-care-and-capital-the-changing-face-of-indias-healthcare-sector/) . Blackstone has also been linked to bids for Medica Synergie and other regional hospital chains. The firm's stated objective is to build a pan-India hospital platform capable of competing with Apollo Hospitals, Max Healthcare, and Fortis Healthcare — all of which have either received PE investment or are actively seeking capital partnerships  [(Countercurrents)](https://countercurrents.org/2026/05/between-care-and-capital-the-changing-face-of-indias-healthcare-sector/) . The competitive dynamics are intensifying: in the Sahyadri bidding process, Blackstone faced competition from EQT, Temasek, and Manipal Hospitals itself, demonstrating that PE firms are not merely acquiring assets but actively competing with each other for market dominance  [(Countercurrents)](https://countercurrents.org/2026/05/between-care-and-capital-the-changing-face-of-indias-healthcare-sector/) .

### 2.2 KKR's Multi-Layered Healthcare Portfolio

KKR & Co., with **$744 billion in AUM**  [(Praxis Rock)](https://praxisrock.com/insights/top-private-equity-firms) , has constructed the most diversified healthcare portfolio of any US PE firm in India, spanning hospitals, pharmaceuticals, medical devices, and health technology. The firm's strategy reflects a thematic conviction in India's healthcare sector, with investments totalling well over **$5 billion** across multiple sub-sectors  [(Simpson Thacher & Bartlett LLP)](https://www.stblaw.com/about-us/news/view/2025/02/24/kkr-to-acquire-healthcare-global-enterprises) .

#### 2.2.1 HCG Cancer Chain Acquisition ($400 Million)

In February 2025, KKR announced the acquisition of up to **54% of Healthcare Global Enterprises (HCG)**, India's largest oncology hospital chain, from CVC Capital Partners' Asia V fund at a purchase price of INR 445 per share — a transaction valued at approximately **$400 million**  [(Precedence Research)](https://www.precedenceresearch.com/news/kkr-acquires-majority-stake-healthcare-global-400m) . Following an open offer to public shareholders mandated by SEBI's Takeover Regulations, KKR's stake could increase to between **54% and 77%**, giving the firm full operational control  [(Simpson Thacher & Bartlett LLP)](https://www.stblaw.com/about-us/news/view/2025/02/24/kkr-to-acquire-healthcare-global-enterprises) . HCG, founded in 1989 by oncologist Dr. B.S. Ajaikumar, operates **25 cancer care centres across 19 cities** with 2,500 beds, nearly 100 operating theatres, and 40 linear accelerator machines  [(Simpson Thacher & Bartlett LLP)](https://www.stblaw.com/about-us/news/view/2025/02/24/kkr-to-acquire-healthcare-global-enterprises) . The acquisition is particularly significant because cancer care is a high-margin, high-growth specialty in India's healthcare market, with HCG having outpaced the industry's 17% compound annual growth rate  [(The Indian Practitioner)](https://theindianpractitioner.com/kkr-nears-acquisition-of-hcg-paving-the-way-for-expansion-in-cancer-care/) . KKR's investment thesis centres on expanding HCG's presence in non-metro cities and funding acquisitions — the chain had already acquired a 53% stake in Mahatma Gandhi Cancer Hospital in Vizag for an enterprise value of ₹4,140 million  [(HCG Oncology)](https://www.hcgoncology.com/uploads/ir/15.%20HCG%20Annual%20Report%20FY2025.pdf) .

#### 2.2.2 Baby Memorial Hospital and Regional Chain Control

In July 2024, KKR acquired up to **65% of Baby Memorial Hospital (BMH)**, a multi-specialty chain in Kerala, for approximately **$280–300 million**  [(practical-patient-care.com)](https://www.practical-patient-care.com/news/kkr-to-acquire-controlling-stake-in-bmh/) . Founded in 1987, BMH operates 1,000 beds across hospitals in Calicut and Kannur, with specialisations in cardiology, oncology, neurology, and orthopaedics  [(practical-patient-care.com)](https://www.practical-patient-care.com/news/kkr-to-acquire-controlling-stake-in-bmh/) . This marked KKR's return to the Indian hospital sector after exiting Max Healthcare in 2022 with returns of approximately **5x** its initial investment  [(practical-patient-care.com)](https://www.practical-patient-care.com/news/kkr-to-acquire-controlling-stake-in-bmh/) . The BMH acquisition reflects a strategic focus on regional hospital chains with strong local brand recognition and expansion potential — KKR explicitly stated its intent to support BMH's goal of building a **pan-India network** through organic and inorganic growth  [(bwhealthcareworld.com)](https://www.bwhealthcareworld.com/article/kkr-acquires-stake-in-baby-memorial-hospital-to-boost-healthcare-in-india-524780) .

#### 2.2.3 Healthium Medtech and Medical Device Dominance

In May 2024, KKR acquired a controlling stake in **Healthium Medtech**, India's leading domestic medical devices company, from UK-based Apax Partners in a deal valued at approximately **$840 million** (₹70 billion)  [(NS Medical Devices)](https://www.nsmedicaldevices.com/news/investment-firm-kkr-to-acquire-india-based-healthium-medtech/) . Healthium, founded in 1992, manufactures surgical products including wound closure devices, arthroscopy equipment, and advanced wound care products used in **one in five surgeries globally**  [(NS Medical Devices)](https://www.nsmedicaldevices.com/news/investment-firm-kkr-to-acquire-india-based-healthium-medtech/) . Under Apax's ownership since 2018, Healthium had expanded from 50 to over 90 countries; KKR's acquisition represents a strategic bet on India's growing medical device export potential and import substitution opportunity  [(Inc42)](https://inc42.com/buzz/kkr-to-buy-indias-medical-devices-firm-healthium-medtech/) . The transaction was funded through KKR's Asian Fund IV and represents one of the largest medtech buyouts in Indian history.

#### 2.2.4 Infinx and Healthcare Technology Investments

KKR has also invested in healthcare technology platforms, acquiring a significant minority stake in **Infinx Services**, an AI-driven healthcare revenue cycle management provider, for approximately **$150 million** in 2024  [(databiztimes.com)](https://databiztimes.com/ai-powered-healthcare-revenue-solution-provider-infinx-secures-150-million-investment-from-kkr/) . Infinx's platform serves 172,000 healthcare professionals across 4,000 facilities, processing over $8 billion in recovered revenue  [(databiztimes.com)](https://databiztimes.com/ai-powered-healthcare-revenue-solution-provider-infinx-secures-150-million-investment-from-kkr/) . While Infinx is headquartered in San Jose, California, its significant India-based operations and development centre make it a relevant component of KKR's India healthcare thesis. The investment aligns with a broader trend of PE firms acquiring healthcare IT and revenue cycle management assets that service the US market from Indian delivery centres.

#### 2.2.5 The JB Pharma Story: Acquisition, Growth, and Exit

KKR's most lucrative Indian healthcare investment was its acquisition of **JB Chemicals and Pharmaceuticals** in 2020 and subsequent sale to Torrent Pharmaceuticals in 2025 for **$3.1 billion** (₹25,689 crore)  [(Alternatives Watch)](https://www.alternativeswatch.com/2025/07/02/kkr-launches-singapore-financial-advisory-platform-exits-india-pharma-investment/) . KKR acquired JB Pharma during the pandemic and, during its five-year ownership, supported the company's expansion in market share, product portfolio, and profitability  [(Alternatives Watch)](https://www.alternativeswatch.com/2025/07/02/kkr-launches-singapore-financial-advisory-platform-exits-india-pharma-investment/) . The exit to Torrent — which acquired up to 49.19% through a share purchase agreement triggering a mandatory open offer of 26% — represented one of the largest pharma exits in Indian PE history and demonstrated the attractiveness of India's branded formulations market to both PE investors and strategic acquirers  [(Alternatives Watch)](https://www.alternativeswatch.com/2025/07/02/kkr-launches-singapore-financial-advisory-platform-exits-india-pharma-investment/) .

### 2.3 TPG and the Evercare Health Fund: A Controversial Model

TPG Inc., managing approximately **$303 billion in AUM**  [(Praxis Rock)](https://praxisrock.com/insights/top-private-equity-firms) , operates one of the most distinctive — and controversial — healthcare investment platforms in emerging markets through its **Evercare Health Fund**, a $1 billion vehicle focused on Africa and South Asia  [(preqin.com)](http://preqin.com/data/profile/asset/evercare-group/489893) . The fund's investments in India have drawn scrutiny from civil society organisations and academic researchers for their alleged impact on healthcare accessibility and affordability.

#### 2.3.1 The Evercare Platform and Care Hospitals Ownership

TPG acquired the Evercare Health Fund in 2019 from the collapsed Abraaj Group, assuming responsibility for a portfolio that included CARE Hospitals in India  [(AVCJ)](https://www.avcj.com/avcj/news/3030016/indian-regulator-approves-blackstones-purchase-of-care-hospitals) . The fund's investors include an array of development finance institutions (DFIs) such as the **U.S. International Development Finance Corporation (DFC)**, the **World Bank's International Finance Corporation (IFC)**, **British International Investment**, and **Proparco** (France), alongside the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, Philips, and Medtronic  [(Private Equity Stakeholder Project PESP)](https://pestakeholder.org/news/private-equity-is-buying-up-hospitals-in-low-and-middle-income-countries-with-the-help-of-development-finance-institutions/) . This structure — in which taxpayer-backed development funds from multiple countries channel capital into a PE-owned hospital platform — has drawn sharp criticism from organisations including Oxfam International.

#### 2.3.2 DFI-Backed Investments and the Oxfam "Sick Development" Report

Oxfam's June 2023 report, **"Sick Development: How rich-country government and World Bank funding to for-profit private hospitals causes harm and should be stopped"**, identified Evercare's CARE Hospitals in India as a case study in how DFI-backed PE investments can undermine universal health coverage  [(Bond)](https://www.bond.org.uk/news/2023/07/sick-development-how-funding-to-for-profit-healthcare-by-development-finance-institutions-is-causing-harm-and-should-be-stopped/) . The report documented patient stories from India, Kenya, and Nigeria, including allegations of **denial of emergency treatment**, **patients being blocked from using government health insurance cards**, and **catastrophic medical bills** that pushed families into poverty  [(Bond)](https://www.bond.org.uk/news/2023/07/sick-development-how-funding-to-for-profit-healthcare-by-development-finance-institutions-is-causing-harm-and-should-be-stopped/) . In India specifically, Oxfam reported cases where patients injured in traffic accidents or demolition incidents were allegedly denied care at DFI-funded private hospitals  [(Bond)](https://www.bond.org.uk/news/2023/07/sick-development-how-funding-to-for-profit-healthcare-by-development-finance-institutions-is-causing-harm-and-should-be-stopped/) . The report recommended that DFIs **cease funding for-profit healthcare providers** and that governments **stop promoting the privatisation of healthcare**  [(Medical Buyer)](https://medicalbuyer.co.in/oxfam-recommends-dfis-and-govt-cease-funding-for-profit-healthcare-providers/) .

#### 2.3.3 Transition to Blackstone and Retained Minority Stakes

When Blackstone acquired CARE Hospitals from TPG in 2023, TPG retained approximately **25% through its Evercare Fund**, ensuring continued exposure to the platform's financial performance  [(mergersindia.com)](https://mnacritique.mergersindia.com/news/blackstone-to-buy-controlling-stakes-in-care-hospitals-kims-health-for-1-bn/) . This arrangement — in which the previous PE owner maintains a minority stake while the new owner assumes control — has become a common feature of Indian hospital transactions, creating layered ownership structures that can obscure ultimate beneficial ownership and accountability. TPG has since redirected its Indian healthcare focus toward other assets, including Columbia Asia Hospitals (acquired in partnership with Hong Leong Group for $1.2 billion in 2019) and digital health platforms  [(Private Equity Stakeholder Project PESP)](https://pestakeholder.org/news/private-equity-is-buying-up-hospitals-in-low-and-middle-income-countries-with-the-help-of-development-finance-institutions/) .

### 2.4 Carlyle's Pharmaceutical and Diagnostic Bets

The Carlyle Group, with approximately **$477 billion in AUM**  [(Praxis Rock)](https://praxisrock.com/insights/top-private-equity-firms) , has pursued a differentiated strategy in India, focusing on pharmaceutical services, diagnostics, and specialty hospital assets rather than large hospital chains.

#### 2.4.1 Piramal Pharma Investment ($490 Million for 20% Stake)

In June 2020, Carlyle agreed to invest approximately **$490 million** for a **20% stake** in Piramal Pharma Limited, the pharmaceutical subsidiary of Piramal Enterprises, at an enterprise valuation of **$2.775 billion**  [(Carlyle)](https://www.carlyle.com/media-room/news-release-archive/carlyle-and-piramal-pharma-sign-agreement-20-strategic-growth) . The transaction was structured as fresh equity capital to accelerate Piramal Pharma's organic and inorganic growth plans across its contract development and manufacturing (CDMO) business, complex hospital generics, consumer products division, and ophthalmology joint venture with Allergan  [(Carlyle)](https://www.carlyle.com/media-room/news-release-archive/carlyle-and-piramal-pharma-sign-agreement-20-strategic-growth) . Carlyle's investment thesis centred on India's growing role as a global pharmaceutical manufacturing hub and Piramal's established position in high-value CDMO services  [(Carlyle)](https://www.carlyle.com/media-room/news-release-archive/carlyle-and-piramal-pharma-sign-agreement-20-strategic-growth) . The deal was one of the largest private equity transactions in the Indian pharmaceutical sector at the time of announcement.

#### 2.4.2 SeQuent Scientific, Medanta, and Metropolis Investments

Carlyle's other Indian healthcare investments include a majority stake in **SeQuent Scientific**, the largest Indian pure-play animal healthcare company; **Medanta Medicity Hospital**, a leading facility in the National Capital Region; and **Metropolis Healthcare**, a chain of diagnostic centres and laboratories  [(Carlyle)](https://www.carlyle.com/media-room/news-release-archive/carlyle-and-piramal-pharma-sign-agreement-20-strategic-growth) . These investments reflect Carlyle's strategy of building a diversified healthcare platform spanning human health, animal health, and diagnostics — sectors that offer both defensive revenue characteristics and growth potential driven by India's rising healthcare spending.

### 2.5 Advent International: Record Returns from Pharma

Advent International, a Boston-based PE firm with over **$6 billion committed to India** across 19 companies  [(The Hindu BusinessLine)](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/companies/pe-firm-advent-to-return-a-record-25000-cr-this-year-from-india-portfolio/article68996646.ece) , has generated some of the highest returns in the Indian healthcare sector, culminating in a record **₹25,000 crore ($3 billion)** returned to limited partners from its India portfolio in 2024 alone  [(The Hindu BusinessLine)](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/companies/pe-firm-advent-to-return-a-record-25000-cr-this-year-from-india-portfolio/article68996646.ece) .

#### 2.5.1 Bharat Serums: From $500M Entry to $1.63B Exit

Advent acquired **Bharat Serums and Vaccines (BSV)** in 2019 for approximately **$500 million** from OrbiMed and Kotak Private Equity, then transformed the company through operational improvements, R&D investment, and margin expansion from 20% to over 30%  [(Advent International)](https://www.adventinternational.com/portfolio-stories/breathing-new-life-into-bharat-serums-and-vaccines-bsv/) . In July 2024, Advent sold BSV to Indian drugmaker **Mankind Pharma** for **$1.63 billion** (₹13,630 crore), generating a **3x return** on its initial investment  [(RNM India)](https://www.rnm.in/blog/mankind-pharma-to-buy-bharat-serums-as-advent-international-exits/) . BSV's portfolio in women's health, fertility treatments, and critical care products aligned with Mankind's strategic vision, creating a domestic champion in high-barrier therapeutic areas  [(VCCircle)](https://www.vccircle.com/mankindpharma-to-buy-bharat-serums-as-advent-international-exits) . The transaction was the largest strategic exit of 2024 and demonstrated the viability of building and exiting pharmaceutical assets in India's growing domestic market  [(EY)](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-in/insights/private-equity/documents/ey-private-equity-and-venture-capital-trendbook-2025-v1.pdf) .

#### 2.5.2 Cohance Lifesciences and CDMO Strategy

Advent has also consolidated its position in India's pharmaceutical services sector through **Cohance Lifesciences**, formed by merging portfolio companies RA Chem Pharma, ZCL Chemicals, and Avra Laboratories, with plans to further merge with Suven Pharmaceuticals  [(Advent International)](https://www.adventinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Advent-pharma-services-report.pdf) . This strategy aims to build a major **contract development and manufacturing organization (CDMO)** player capable of serving global pharmaceutical clients from Indian manufacturing facilities. Advent's pharma services investments reflect a broader industry trend of PE firms capitalising on the "China plus one" sourcing strategy, as global drugmakers seek to diversify manufacturing away from over-dependence on Chinese suppliers  [(Advent International)](https://www.adventinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Advent-pharma-services-report.pdf) .

#### 2.5.3 Apollo HealthCo and Consumer Health Investments

In September 2024, Advent invested approximately **$297 million** in **Apollo HealthCo**, the digital pharmacy and healthcare services arm of Apollo Hospitals, acquiring a 17% stake  [(EY)](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-in/insights/private-equity/documents/ey-private-equity-and-venture-capital-trendbook-2025-v1.pdf) . This investment signals PE interest in India's digital health ecosystem, particularly online pharmacies and telemedicine platforms that have experienced rapid growth following the COVID-19 pandemic. Apollo HealthCo's integration with India's largest hospital network creates a unique "omnichannel" healthcare delivery model that combines physical infrastructure with digital distribution.

## 3. Pharmaceutical Sector: APIs, Formulations, and the Control of Medicine

The Indian pharmaceutical industry — valued at approximately **$45 billion** in 2025 and projected to reach **$130 billion by 2030**  [(网易)](https://www.163.com/dy/article/JEL34VV60530UH99.html)  — has attracted substantial US PE investment across the value chain, from active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) manufacturing to branded formulations and contract research services. Unlike the hospital sector, where investments are primarily domestic-market focused, pharma investments often serve global supply chains, raising distinct questions about the strategic implications of foreign ownership over India's "pharmacy to the world" role.

### 3.1 KKR's JB Pharma: The $3.1 Billion Exit

KKR's investment in **JB Chemicals and Pharmaceuticals** exemplifies the PE value-creation model in Indian pharma. Acquired in 2020 at the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, JB Pharma was scaled up under KKR's ownership through product expansion, market share gains, and profitability improvements  [(Alternatives Watch)](https://www.alternativeswatch.com/2025/07/02/kkr-launches-singapore-financial-advisory-platform-exits-india-pharma-investment/) . In mid-2025, KKR agreed to sell its controlling stake to **Torrent Pharmaceuticals** at an equity valuation of **₹25,689 crore ($3.1 billion)** on a fully diluted basis  [(Alternatives Watch)](https://www.alternativeswatch.com/2025/07/02/kkr-launches-singapore-financial-advisory-platform-exits-india-pharma-investment/) . Torrent's acquisition — which triggers a mandatory open offer of 26% under SEBI regulations — will merge JB Pharma into Torrent's operations, creating a stronger domestic player in branded formulations  [(Alternatives Watch)](https://www.alternativeswatch.com/2025/07/02/kkr-launches-singapore-financial-advisory-platform-exits-india-pharma-investment/) . The transaction, subject to approvals from SEBI, CCI, and the National Company Law Tribunal, represents one of the largest M&A deals in Indian pharma history and validates KKR's strategy of building pharmaceutical platforms for strategic exits.

### 3.2 Advent's Bharat Serums: Women's Health and Fertility Dominance

Advent International's transformation of **Bharat Serums and Vaccines** into a women's health and fertility powerhouse before selling to Mankind Pharma for **$1.63 billion** illustrates how PE firms can reshape therapeutic focus through capital allocation and management changes  [(Advent International)](https://www.adventinternational.com/portfolio-stories/breathing-new-life-into-bharat-serums-and-vaccines-bsv/) . Under Advent's ownership, BSV built an expert management team, professional board structure, robust R&D pipeline with over 60 scientists, and a global footprint spanning 70+ countries  [(Advent International)](https://www.adventinternational.com/portfolio-stories/breathing-new-life-into-bharat-serums-and-vaccines-bsv/) . The company's margins expanded from 20% at entry to over 30% at exit, driven by pricing power in niche therapeutic areas including assisted reproductive treatment and immunoglobulin products  [(Advent International)](https://www.adventinternational.com/portfolio-stories/breathing-new-life-into-bharat-serums-and-vaccines-bsv/) . Mankind Pharma's acquisition positioned it as the market leader in Indian women's health — a significant strategic outcome that concentrated market power in a high-growth therapeutic segment  [(RNM India)](https://www.rnm.in/blog/mankind-pharma-to-buy-bharat-serums-as-advent-international-exits/) .

### 3.3 Carlyle's Piramal Pharma: CDMO and Complex Generics

Carlyle's **$490 million investment** in Piramal Pharma for a 20% stake was structured around the thesis that India's contract development and manufacturing organisation (CDMO) sector would benefit from global pharmaceutical companies diversifying supply chains away from China  [(Carlyle)](https://www.carlyle.com/media-room/news-release-archive/carlyle-and-piramal-pharma-sign-agreement-20-strategic-growth) . Piramal Pharma Solutions, the CDMO division, provides end-to-end services from drug development to commercial manufacturing for global clients. The investment also encompassed Piramal Critical Care (complex hospital generics sold across 100+ countries), consumer products, and an ophthalmology joint venture with Allergan  [(Carlyle)](https://www.carlyle.com/media-room/news-release-archive/carlyle-and-piramal-pharma-sign-agreement-20-strategic-growth) . Carlyle's global healthcare experience — including prior investments in Albany Molecular Research (AMRI), PPD, and Ambio — was cited as a key value-add for Piramal's international expansion  [(Carlyle)](https://www.carlyle.com/media-room/news-release-archive/carlyle-and-piramal-pharma-sign-agreement-20-strategic-growth) .

### 3.4 The API Dependency Question: Chinese Inputs, Foreign Ownership

A critical dimension of pharmaceutical sector PE investment that receives insufficient attention is the **structural dependency on Chinese inputs** for active pharmaceutical ingredients and key starting materials (KSMs). While PE firms invest in Indian pharma companies to capitalise on domestic market growth and export opportunities, these companies themselves remain heavily reliant on Chinese supply chains. According to PwC analysis, approximately **50% of critical APIs** used in India are imported, and **almost all of these imports originate from China**  [(PwC India)](https://www.pwc.in/assets/pdfs/industries/pharmaceuticals-and-life-sciences/reviving-indias-api-industry.pdf) . The percentage of API imports from China spiked from around 1% in 1991 to approximately **70% in 2019**  [(PwC India)](https://www.pwc.in/assets/pdfs/industries/pharmaceuticals-and-life-sciences/reviving-indias-api-industry.pdf) . Key starting materials for essential drugs — including penicillin G, amoxicillin, azithromycin, metformin, and cephalosporins — continue to be sourced primarily from Chinese manufacturers  [(PwC India)](https://www.pwc.in/assets/pdfs/industries/pharmaceuticals-and-life-sciences/reviving-indias-api-industry.pdf) . This creates a paradox: US PE firms are acquiring ownership of Indian pharmaceutical companies that, in turn, depend on Chinese inputs, layering foreign ownership over an already vulnerable supply chain. The Indian government's Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme for Bulk Drugs, launched in 2020, has made progress in establishing domestic manufacturing capacity for 26 KSMs/APIs, with cumulative sales of ₹2,315 crore including exports of ₹508 crore  [(TransData Nexus)](https://transdatanexus.com/pharmaceutical/ibuprofen/) . However, the scheme primarily targets antibiotics, leaving other therapeutic areas — including oncology drugs and complex formulations — still exposed to Chinese supply chain disruptions.

## 4. Food and Agriculture: The New Frontier for PE Capital

India's food processing sector — the world's largest by production volume, valued at approximately **$535 billion** and growing at **8.3% annually**  [(MITCON Consultancy & Engineering Services Limited)](https://www.mitconindia.com/blogs/potential-of-the-indian-food-processing-sector/)  — has emerged as a major destination for private equity investment. The Indian government's decision to allow **100% FDI** in food processing, combined with a $13.49 billion cumulative FDI inflow since 2000  [(Invest India)](https://www.investindia.gov.in/sector/food-processing) , has created favourable conditions for PE firms to acquire stakes in iconic Indian food brands and agricultural supply chains.

### 4.1 Haldiram's: The $10 Billion Snack Empire

The most prominent example of PE penetration into India's food sector is **Haldiram's Snacks Food Private Limited**, the iconic ethnic snacks and sweets brand founded in 1937 in Bikaner, Rajasthan. In 2025, Haldiram's became the subject of an intense bidding war among the world's largest investment firms, ultimately achieving a valuation of approximately **$10 billion** (₹89,000 crore) — making it one of the most valuable privately held food companies globally  [(The Economic Times)](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/temasek-signs-agreement-to-buy-10-in-haldiram-snacks-food/articleshow/118946807.cms) .

#### 4.1.1 The Bidding War: Temasek, Blackstone, Bain, and Alpha Wave

The race for Haldiram's stake attracted an extraordinary roster of global investors. Three consortiums submitted binding offers for a 10–15% stake: a **Blackstone-led group** with Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA) and Singapore's GIC; a **Temasek-Bain Capital combine** (which later split, with Bain dropping out); and **Alpha Wave Global**  [(The Economic Times)](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/temasek-signs-agreement-to-buy-10-in-haldiram-snacks-food/articleshow/118946807.cms) . Blackstone ultimately withdrew after seven months of negotiations, citing valuation concerns — the firm had valued Haldiram's at approximately **$8 billion**, while the Agarwal family promoters sought **$10–11 billion**  [(MarketScreener.com)](https://www.marketscreener.com/quote/stock/BLACKSTONE-INC-60951400/news/Blackstone-Reportedly-Out-of-Race-for-India-s-Haldiram-s-over-Valuation-49244075/) . Additional disconnect emerged over IPO timeline: Blackstone wanted Haldiram's to list within three years, while the family preferred five years  [(MarketScreener.com)](https://www.marketscreener.com/quote/stock/BLACKSTONE-INC-60951400/news/Blackstone-Reportedly-Out-of-Race-for-India-s-Haldiram-s-over-Valuation-49244075/) . **Temasek** ultimately secured approximately **10% for $1 billion** (₹8,000 crore)  [(The Economic Times)](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/temasek-signs-agreement-to-buy-10-in-haldiram-snacks-food/articleshow/118946807.cms) , followed by **Alpha Wave Global** acquiring 6% for ₹5,600 crore  [(prestairsystem.com)](https://prestairsystem.com/alpha-wave-acquires-6-stake-in-haldirams-for-rs-5600-crore-paving-the-way-for-ipo/)  and the **UAE's International Holding Company (IHC)** taking a stake. In December 2025, **L Catterton** — the LVMH-backed US consumer-focused PE firm managing $39 billion — announced a strategic partnership and minority investment, with CCI approval following in February 2026  [(storyboard18.com)](https://www.storyboard18.com/how-it-works/cci-clears-l-catterton-india-funds-minority-stake-acquisition-in-haldirams-88936.htm) .

#### 4.1.2 Valuation Debate: Is a $10 Billion Indian Snack Company Justified?

The $10 billion valuation for Haldiram's — which generated approximately **₹12,800 crore ($1.5 billion)** in revenue in FY24  [(The Economic Times)](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/temasek-signs-agreement-to-buy-10-in-haldiram-snacks-food/articleshow/118946807.cms)  — implies a revenue multiple of roughly **6.7x**, comparable to global FMCG peers but aggressive for an Indian consumer company. The valuation was justified by Haldiram's dominant market position: Euromonitor International estimates the brand holds approximately **13% of India's $6.2 billion savoury snacks market**, on par with PepsiCo's Lay's  [(theedgemalaysia.com)](https://theedgemalaysia.com/node/747701) . The company's 500+ product SKUs span snacks, sweets, ready-to-eat foods, dairy, bakery, chocolates, and beverages, distributed across **7 million retail outlets** in India and **80+ countries** internationally  [(The Indian Express)](https://indianexpress.com/article/business/lvmh-backed-private-equity-firm-l-catterton-haldirams-stake-10429378/) . However, critics noted that Haldiram's had attracted suitors including General Atlantic, TA Associates, Warburg Pincus, Everstone, Capital International, PepsiCo, Kellogg's, and Tata Consumer Products over nearly a decade — many of whom walked away due to valuation disagreements or the family's changing stance  [(The Economic Times)](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/10-billion-bhujia-whats-behind-haldirams-stratospheric-valuation/articleshow/118971313.cms) .

#### 4.1.3 L Catterton Entry and the LVMH Connection

L Catterton's investment in Haldiram's — approved by CCI in February 2026 — is particularly notable because the firm is backed by **LVMH Moët Hennessy Louis Vuitton**, the world's largest luxury goods conglomerate  [(storyboard18.com)](https://www.storyboard18.com/how-it-works/cci-clears-l-catterton-india-funds-minority-stake-acquisition-in-haldirams-88936.htm) . L Catterton's India operations are headed by **Sanjiv Mehta**, former CEO and Managing Director of Hindustan Unilever, India's largest FMCG company  [(The Indian Express)](https://indianexpress.com/article/business/lvmh-backed-private-equity-firm-l-catterton-haldirams-stake-10429378/) . The firm's global food investments include Cholula Hot Sauce, Ferrara Candy Company, Kettle Foods, and Kodiak Cakes  [(storyboard18.com)](https://www.storyboard18.com/how-it-works/cci-clears-l-catterton-india-funds-minority-stake-acquisition-in-haldirams-88936.htm) . L Catterton's strategic partnership with Haldiram's focuses on **brand building, new product development, supply chain optimisation, geographic expansion, and talent development** — a classic PE value-creation playbook applied to an Indian heritage brand  [(The Indian Express)](https://indianexpress.com/article/business/lvmh-backed-private-equity-firm-l-catterton-haldirams-stake-10429378/) . The entry of an LVMH-linked firm into India's largest ethnic snacks company raises questions about whether global luxury and consumer conglomerates are using PE vehicles to gain footholds in India's food sector without triggering direct acquisition scrutiny.

### 4.2 Capital Foods and the Ching's Secret Exit

In 2024, **Invus Group** and **General Atlantic** exited their investment in **Capital Foods**, the maker of Ching's Secret and Smith & Jones brands, by selling a 75% stake to **Tata Consumer Products** for **$460 million**  [(EY)](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-in/insights/private-equity/documents/ey-private-equity-and-venture-capital-trendbook-2025-v1.pdf) . The transaction was the third-largest strategic exit of 2024 and demonstrated the viability of building branded food companies in India for PE-backed exits to strategic acquirers. Capital Foods had created a dominant position in the ethnic Chinese and Indian fusion cooking sauces segment, a niche that Tata Consumer sought to complement its existing portfolio of tea, coffee, and salt products.

### 4.3 Advanta Seeds and Agricultural Inputs

Alpha Wave Global's **$350 million investment** in **Advanta Enterprises (Advanta Seeds)** in 2024 represented one of the largest agribusiness deals of the year  [(EY)](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-in/insights/private-equity/documents/ey-private-equity-and-venture-capital-trendbook-2025-v1.pdf) . Advanta, a leading seed company with a global presence, produces hybrid seeds for crops including corn, sunflower, sorghum, and vegetables. The investment reflects PE interest in India's agricultural input sector, which is seen as critical to improving farm productivity and food security. However, foreign ownership of seed companies raises distinct regulatory sensitivities in India, where farmer protests in 2020–2021 against corporate agriculture laws demonstrated deep public opposition to perceived corporate control over farming inputs.

### 4.4 The 100% FDI Policy and Its Implications

In January 2025, India's Minister of Commerce and Industry **Piyush Goyal** announced that the food and beverage industry would allow **100% foreign direct investment** — a sweeping change that could accelerate PE acquisition activity  [(asianinsiders.com)](https://asianinsiders.com/2025/03/04/indian-food-and-beverage-targets-foreign-investment-as-part-of-ambitious-growth-plan/) . While overseas ownership had previously been permitted in food processing, the comprehensive liberalisation removed remaining restrictions and signalled India's ambition to attract foreign capital to achieve its target of **$100 billion in combined exports** from food, agriculture, and marine products by 2030  [(asianinsiders.com)](https://asianinsiders.com/2025/03/04/indian-food-and-beverage-targets-foreign-investment-as-part-of-ambitious-growth-plan/) . The policy change aligns with India's broader economic strategy of positioning itself as a global food processing hub, but critics argue that unrestricted foreign ownership of food brands and agricultural supply chains could compromise domestic food security and farmer livelihoods.

## 5. The Dark Side: Rising Costs, Patient Harms, and Systemic Risks

While PE firms and their portfolio companies publicly emphasise improved efficiency, expanded access, and quality enhancements, a substantial body of evidence — spanning academic research, investigative journalism, patient advocacy reports, and regulatory complaints — documents significant adverse consequences of PE ownership in Indian healthcare. These harms are not incidental byproducts but structural outcomes of the PE business model, which prioritises **financial returns over patient welfare** and operates on **3–7 year investment horizons** that incentivise aggressive cost-cutting and revenue maximisation  [(Center for American Progress)](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/5-consequences-of-private-equitys-expansion-in-health-care-services/) .

### 5.1 The UPenn CASI Study: Equity versus Access

Research from the **University of Pennsylvania's Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI)** provides the most comprehensive academic analysis of PE's impact on Indian healthcare. The study documents that while PE investments have expanded physical infrastructure — with **22,000 new hospital beds** expected to be added in private hospitals over the next 3–5 years, tripling the number added between 2019 and 2024 — this expansion has been **concentrated in high-margin services and urban areas**  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) . PE-backed hospital chains like Manipal, Fortis, and Care have expanded primarily in Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities, but the services offered focus on tertiary care, specialised surgeries, and diagnostic procedures that generate the highest returns — rather than primary care, preventive services, or rural health access  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) . The study notes that **out-of-pocket health expenditure in India constituted approximately 54.8% of current health expenditure in 2024** — one of the highest rates globally — and that healthcare expenses pushed **8–9% of all Indian households below the poverty line** in 2023  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) .

### 5.2 The Cost Explosion: Private vs Public Hospital Charges

The most direct and measurable impact of PE-driven hospital consolidation has been the **dramatic widening of the cost gap** between private and public healthcare facilities. According to National Sample Survey data cited in the CASI study, hospitalisation in private hospitals cost **2.3 times more than public hospitals in rural areas and 3.1 times more in urban areas** in 1986–87  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) . By 2017–18, these gaps had widened to **6.4 times in rural areas and 8.0 times in urban areas**  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) . This cost explosion has occurred concurrently with the expansion of corporate hospital chains and PE investment — though causation is complex, the correlation is striking. PE-backed hospitals consistently achieve higher **Average Revenue Per Occupied Bed (ARPOB)** than non-PE facilities: while volume-based models like Narayana Health maintain ARPOB of ₹30,000–32,000, premium chains like Apollo Hospitals achieve ₹55,000–60,000, and high-specialty PE-backed facilities command up to **35x Enterprise Value/EBITDA multiples**  [(Dealflow IQ)](https://www.dealflowiq.com/p/the-india-hospitals-report-2025-capital) . The business model inherently rewards maximising revenue per patient rather than minimising costs for patients.

### 5.3 Oxfam's "Sick Development": Patient Abuses at DFI-Backed Hospitals

Oxfam International's **"Sick Development"** report, published in June 2023, provided the most damning evidence of patient harm at PE-backed hospitals in developing countries, including India  [(Bond)](https://www.bond.org.uk/news/2023/07/sick-development-how-funding-to-for-profit-healthcare-by-development-finance-institutions-is-causing-harm-and-should-be-stopped/) . The report documented how Development Finance Institutions (DFIs) — including the World Bank's IFC, the U.S. DFC, and European DFIs — channel taxpayer money into PE-owned hospital chains that then engage in practices directly contrary to the Sustainable Development Goal of universal health coverage. In India, Oxfam investigators found cases of **emergency patients being denied treatment** — including a child badly injured in a traffic accident, a stab victim, and a woman crushed when bulldozers demolished her informal settlement  [(Bond)](https://www.bond.org.uk/news/2023/07/sick-development-how-funding-to-for-profit-healthcare-by-development-finance-institutions-is-causing-harm-and-should-be-stopped/) . Patients reported being **blocked from using government health insurance cards** (entitling them to free care under schemes like Ayushman Bharat) and instead being charged fees that pushed families into debt  [(Bond)](https://www.bond.org.uk/news/2023/07/sick-development-how-funding-to-for-profit-healthcare-by-development-finance-institutions-is-causing-harm-and-should-be-stopped/) . A woman from an informal settlement in Odisha described a DFI-funded corporate hospital in stark terms: *"When they learn that we are from the slum the hospital staff make us leave…we don't take people there now… It is not for us. It is not for the poor families. It is for the rich people"*  [(Bond)](https://www.bond.org.uk/news/2023/07/sick-development-how-funding-to-for-profit-healthcare-by-development-finance-institutions-is-causing-harm-and-should-be-stopped/) .

### 5.4 Counter Currents Investigation: Malpractice Allegations

An investigative report published in Counter Currents in May 2026 documented **"rising allegations of malpractices at the so-called large hospital chains"** under PE ownership  [(Countercurrents)](https://countercurrents.org/2026/05/between-care-and-capital-the-changing-face-of-indias-healthcare-sector/) . The report cited patient and consumer forum complaints about **price-related issues, unwanted diagnostic tests, inflated bills, and efforts by hospital authorities to ensure prolonged stays** that result in larger bills  [(Countercurrents)](https://countercurrents.org/2026/05/between-care-and-capital-the-changing-face-of-indias-healthcare-sector/) . The investigation noted that "hospitals working under the pressure of private equity investors have removed the thin line between quality care and commercial exploitation resulting in financial devastation for patients"  [(Countercurrents)](https://countercurrents.org/2026/05/between-care-and-capital-the-changing-face-of-indias-healthcare-sector/) . These allegations align with academic research from the United States, where a systematic review of 47 studies found that PE ownership of healthcare facilities was "largely associated with lower performance on health system aims including patient health outcomes, process quality, and costs for patients and other payers"  [(HMPI)](https://hmpi.org/2024/06/19/an-update-on-impacts-of-private-equity-ownership-in-health-care-extending-a-systematic-review/) .

### 5.5 The Academic Consensus: Financialisation and Its Discontents

A landmark academic paper published in PMC, **"Financialisation and the Reshaping of Private Healthcare,"** examined how financialisation has transformed India's private healthcare system  [(nih.gov)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12082753/) . The study found that PE investment has encouraged the growth of **corporate forms of healthcare that distort or crowd out other forms of provision**, reshaping care processes, professional dynamics, access to services, and regulatory systems  [(nih.gov)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12082753/) . The paper highlighted the "trickle-down" of financialisation from global capital markets to Indian healthcare delivery, demonstrating how financial motives and values increasingly dominate clinical decision-making  [(nih.gov)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12082753/) . A separate systematic review of PE ownership in US healthcare found consistent evidence of **increased charges, higher negotiated prices, reduced staffing ratios, and mixed effects on quality** — patterns that appear to be replicating in the Indian context  [(NIHCM)](https://nihcm.org/publications/the-growth-of-private-equity-in-us-health-care-impact-and-outlook) .

## 6. Regulatory Framework: FDI Policy, Competition Law, and Oversight Gaps

India's regulatory framework for foreign investment in healthcare and food processing is characterised by **liberal FDI policies** combined with **fragmented oversight mechanisms** that struggle to keep pace with the scale and complexity of PE transactions. While the entry of foreign capital is actively encouraged, the downstream consequences — for patient welfare, market competition, and economic sovereignty — receive inadequate scrutiny.

### 6.1 100% FDI Automatic Route for Hospitals

Under India's Consolidated FDI Policy overseen by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT), **up to 100% FDI is permitted under the automatic route** for investments in the hospital segment  [(Lexology)](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=43f53311-5aaf-42f7-b869-ec86ed9a0b38) . This means foreign investors — including US PE firms — can acquire complete ownership of Indian hospital chains without requiring government approval. The policy, designed to attract capital for healthcare infrastructure expansion, has been highly effective: FDI inflows into the hospital sector reached **$3.2 billion between 2022 and 2024**, and in FY 2024–25 alone, the sector received **$3 billion in FDI**  [(Dealflow IQ)](https://www.dealflowiq.com/p/the-india-hospitals-report-2025-capital) . Between April 2000 and June 2020, India received **$6.8 billion in FDI** specifically in hospitals and diagnostic centres  [(NITI Aayog)](https://www.niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2023-02/InvestmentOpportunities_HealthcareSector.pdf) .

### 6.2 Pharma FDI: 74% Automatic, Beyond Requires Approval

The pharmaceutical sector has slightly more restrictive rules: **100% FDI is permitted under the automatic route for greenfield projects**, but for **brownfield (existing) pharmaceutical companies, only 74% FDI is automatic** — any foreign investment exceeding 74% requires government approval through the Foreign Investment Promotion Board  [(Lexology)](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=43f53311-5aaf-42f7-b869-ec86ed9a0b38) . This distinction aims to balance foreign investment with domestic industry protection, though in practice, PE firms have structured transactions to remain below the 74% threshold or have obtained necessary approvals. The brownfield restriction reflects sensitivity about foreign control over India's established pharmaceutical manufacturing base, which supplies approximately **20% of the world's generic medicines** by volume  [(网易)](https://www.163.com/dy/article/JEL34VV60530UH99.html) .

### 6.3 Competition Commission of India: Limited Scrutiny

The Competition Commission of India (CCI) reviews PE transactions that meet certain thresholds, but its mandate focuses on **competition concerns** rather than public health or patient welfare impacts. The CCI approved Blackstone's acquisition of CARE Hospitals in September 2023, noting that the transaction did not raise competition concerns  [(AVCJ)](https://www.avcj.com/avcj/news/3030016/indian-regulator-approves-blackstones-purchase-of-care-hospitals) . Similarly, CCI approved L Catterton's minority stake acquisition in Haldiram's in February 2026  [(storyboard18.com)](https://www.storyboard18.com/how-it-works/cci-clears-l-catterton-india-funds-minority-stake-acquisition-in-haldirams-88936.htm) . However, the CCI does not assess whether hospital acquisitions might lead to price increases, service reductions, or patient harm — dimensions that fall outside competition law but are central to the public interest.

### 6.4 The Oversight Vacuum: No Healthcare-Specific PE Regulation

Perhaps the most significant regulatory gap is the **absence of healthcare-specific oversight** for PE-owned hospital chains. Unlike the United States, where multiple states have introduced legislation to curtail PE's harmful practices in healthcare  [(Private Equity Stakeholder Project PESP)](https://pestakeholder.org/news/private-equity-is-buying-up-hospitals-in-low-and-middle-income-countries-with-the-help-of-development-finance-institutions/) , and where the federal government has signalled increased antitrust focus on PE in healthcare and its role in Medicare/Medicaid fraud  [(Private Equity Stakeholder Project PESP)](https://pestakeholder.org/news/private-equity-is-buying-up-hospitals-in-low-and-middle-income-countries-with-the-help-of-development-finance-institutions/) , India lacks any comparable framework. There is no requirement for PE-owned hospitals to disclose their ownership structure to patients, no restrictions on dividend recapitalisations or debt-funded extractions, no mandated minimum service obligations, and no systematic monitoring of patient outcomes at PE-backed facilities. The Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY public insurance scheme — which covers approximately **550 million beneficiaries** — engages with only **13% of private hospitals** in some states, with PE-backed chains particularly reluctant to participate due to low reimbursement rates  [(Dealflow IQ)](https://www.dealflowiq.com/p/the-india-hospitals-report-2025-capital) . This creates a two-tier system where PE-owned hospitals serve affluent, privately insured patients while public facilities and non-PE private hospitals bear the burden of serving the poor.

## 7. The Food Sector: Concentration, Control, and Consumer Impact

While the adverse effects of PE ownership in healthcare are increasingly documented, the implications of PE penetration into India's food sector remain less studied but potentially equally significant. The food sector affects every Indian household daily, and the concentration of ownership in iconic brands and agricultural supply chains raises questions about food security, pricing power, and cultural autonomy.

### 7.1 From Family-Owned to PE-Backed: The Haldiram's Transformation

Haldiram's journey from a family-owned Bikaner sweet shop founded in 1937 to a **$10 billion PE-backed FMCG giant** encapsulates the broader transformation of India's food sector  [(Outlook Business)](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/corporate/haldirams-10-billion-valuation-what-it-reveals-about-indias-snack-market) . The Agarwal family — which had split the business into Delhi, Nagpur, Kolkata, and Bikaner factions over decades — consolidated the Delhi and Nagpur operations into **Haldiram Snacks Food Pvt Ltd** through a merger approved by the National Company Law Tribunal  [(The Economic Times)](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/10-billion-bhujia-whats-behind-haldirams-stratospheric-valuation/articleshow/118971313.cms) . The restructuring, which created a unified entity with the Delhi group holding 56% and the Nagpur group 44%, was explicitly designed to prepare the company for external investment and a potential IPO  [(The Economic Times)](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/10-billion-bhujia-whats-behind-haldirams-stratospheric-valuation/articleshow/118971313.cms) . The family's decision to bring in a professional CEO in May 2023 — a first for the company — further signalled the transition from family management to institutional ownership  [(The Economic Times)](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/10-billion-bhujia-whats-behind-haldirams-stratospheric-valuation/articleshow/118971313.cms) . The entry of Temasek, Alpha Wave, IHC, and L Catterton has given these global investors board influence, strategic input, and a pathway to exit through IPO or secondary sale — potentially at valuations that prioritise financial returns over preserving the brand's heritage pricing or product accessibility.

### 7.2 PE-Induced Consolidation in Packaged Foods

Beyond Haldiram's, PE firms have driven significant consolidation across India's packaged food landscape. **ChrysCapital** acquired a controlling stake in **Theobroma Foods**, a bakery and café chain  [(Markets Group)](https://www.marketsgroup.org/news/private-equity-accelerates-investment-in-indias-food-beverage-sector) . **Devyani International** (backed by PE investors including Temasek) acquired **Sky Gate Hospitality**, owner of Biryani By Kilo, expanding its QSR portfolio beyond KFC and Pizza Hut  [(Markets Group)](https://www.marketsgroup.org/news/private-equity-accelerates-investment-in-indias-food-beverage-sector) . In 2025, **Adani Wilmar** (itself partially foreign-owned) acquired **G.D. Foods Manufacturing**, known for its Tops brand of sauces and pickles  [(Investment Guru)](https://investmentguruindia.com/newsdetail/pe-deals-in-india-s-consumer-space-rise-38-in-2024-momentum-accelerates-in-2025-269509) . The trend points toward a packaged food sector increasingly controlled by institutional investors — both PE firms and sovereign wealth funds — rather than family promoters or domestic strategic companies. This shift could affect everything from product pricing and ingredient sourcing to distribution priorities and rural market access.

### 7.3 The Quick-Commerce Factor: Accelerating PE Interest

The emergence of quick-commerce platforms — **Zepto, Blinkit, Swiggy Instamart** — has fundamentally altered the food distribution landscape and intensified PE interest in the sector. As Arjun Anand, Managing Director at Verlinvest, noted, quick commerce has "transformed market entry and scaling, allowing brands to launch, test, and expand in months rather than years, creating an ecosystem that is increasingly attractive for private equity deployment"  [(Markets Group)](https://www.marketsgroup.org/news/private-equity-accelerates-investment-in-indias-food-beverage-sector) . Zepto alone raised **$1.4 billion** in 2024, representing 44% of total PE/VC investments in e-commerce  [(EY)](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-in/insights/private-equity/documents/ey-private-equity-and-venture-capital-trendbook-2025-v1.pdf) . The integration of food brands with quick-commerce platforms creates data-driven advantages for PE-owned food companies — they can monitor real-time consumer preferences, optimise pricing dynamically, and achieve national distribution faster than traditional retail-based competitors.

## 8. Synthesis: Where the Evidence Leads

The evidence assembled across this investigation points to a clear and accelerating trend: **US private equity firms are systematically acquiring controlling stakes in India's healthcare, pharmaceutical, and food sectors**, deploying billions of dollars in capital to consolidate ownership of assets that affect the fundamental wellbeing of 1.4 billion people. The question is not whether this takeover is occurring — the transaction data, regulatory filings, and corporate announcements confirm it unambiguously — but what it means for India's economic sovereignty, public health, and food security.

### 8.1 The Scale of the Takeover: By the Numbers

Across the three sectors examined, US PE firms have executed transactions valued at well over **$10 billion** since 2020. In healthcare, Blackstone's Care-KIMS platform ($1B+), KKR's portfolio (HCG at $400M, Baby Memorial at $290M, Healthium at $840M, Infinx at $150M), Carlyle's Piramal Pharma ($490M), and Advent's Apollo HealthCo ($297M) represent direct investments exceeding $3.5 billion — and this excludes KKR's $3.1 billion JB Pharma exit, Advent's $1.63 billion Bharat Serums exit, and the extensive Manipal Hospitals consolidation (₹12,000+ crore) enabled by Temasek and other PE backers  [(Countercurrents)](https://countercurrents.org/2026/05/between-care-and-capital-the-changing-face-of-indias-healthcare-sector/) . In food, Haldiram's at $10 billion valuation with PE investors holding approximately 20%+ collectively, Capital Foods at $460 million, and Advanta Seeds at $350 million add several billion more. The aggregate capital deployed — including co-investments from non-US partners — likely exceeds **$15 billion** across these three sectors alone.

### 8.2 The Business Model: Why India, Why Now?

India's attractiveness to US PE firms stems from a convergence of structural factors. The country's **healthcare market is projected to reach $320 billion by 2028** (from $180 billion in 2023), driven by an ageing population, rising chronic disease burden, and expanding health insurance penetration  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) . The pharmaceutical sector is expected to **double to $130 billion by 2030**  [(网易)](https://www.163.com/dy/article/JEL34VV60530UH99.html) . Food consumption is projected to surpass **$1.2 trillion** within two years  [(asianinsiders.com)](https://asianinsiders.com/2025/03/04/indian-food-and-beverage-targets-foreign-investment-as-part-of-ambitious-growth-plan/) . India's regulatory environment offers **100% FDI automatic route** for hospitals and food processing, minimal PE-specific oversight, and a competitive landscape fragmented enough to enable roll-up strategies  [(Lexology)](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=43f53311-5aaf-42f7-b869-ec86ed9a0b38) . Hospital multiples of **28x Enterprise Value/EBITDA** — with high-ARPOB facilities commanding up to 35x — offer attractive returns compared to developed markets  [(Dealflow IQ)](https://www.dealflowiq.com/p/the-india-hospitals-report-2025-capital) . Perhaps most importantly, India's **public healthcare spending remains abysmally low at 1.2–1.5% of GDP**  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) , far below the 5% recommended for universal health coverage, creating a vacuum that private capital — both domestic and foreign — rushes to fill.

### 8.3 The Consequences: Who Benefits, Who Loses?

The evidence strongly suggests that the primary beneficiaries of PE penetration are the **PE firms themselves, their limited partners (pension funds, endowments, sovereign wealth funds), and the promoters who sell their stakes at inflated valuations**. Advent International returned a record **$3 billion to LPs** from India in 2024 alone, with returns of **3–5x** across multiple exits  [(The Hindu BusinessLine)](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/companies/pe-firm-advent-to-return-a-record-25000-cr-this-year-from-india-portfolio/article68996646.ece) . KKR generated approximately **5x returns** on its Max Healthcare exit  [(practical-patient-care.com)](https://www.practical-patient-care.com/news/kkr-to-acquire-controlling-stake-in-bmh/) . Blackstone has stated that its India investments have yielded "some of the highest returns" globally  [(The Hindu BusinessLine)](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/companies/pe-firm-advent-to-return-a-record-25000-cr-this-year-from-india-portfolio/article68996646.ece) . The promoters of Haldiram's, CARE Hospitals, and Bharat Serums have all become billionaires or near-billionaires through PE-backed transactions.

The losers, according to the evidence, are **Indian patients, particularly the poor and uninsured**. Out-of-pocket expenditure remains at **54.8% of total health spending**  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) . Private hospitals charge **6.4–8.0x more than public facilities**  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) . **8–9% of Indian households** are pushed below the poverty line annually due to healthcare costs  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) . Patient complaints about **inflated bills, unnecessary tests, and denial of care** at PE-backed hospitals are rising  [(Countercurrents)](https://countercurrents.org/2026/05/between-care-and-capital-the-changing-face-of-indias-healthcare-sector/) . The food sector implications are less immediately visible but potentially equally significant: as PE-backed food companies prioritise margin expansion and IPO preparation, product pricing, ingredient quality, and rural distribution may all be affected.

### 8.4 The Strategic Dimension: Healthcare Sovereignty in a Geopolitical Context

Beyond the economic and social implications, the PE takeover of Indian healthcare carries **strategic significance** that deserves attention. India's pharmaceutical industry is the world's largest supplier of generic medicines, and its hospital sector serves a population of 1.4 billion. When ownership of critical healthcare infrastructure — cancer hospitals, multi-specialty chains, medical device manufacturers, and pharmaceutical companies — is concentrated in the hands of US-based investment firms, questions of national interest arise. Unlike Chinese state-owned enterprises, whose investments in strategic sectors trigger immediate security reviews, PE investments flow through structures (Cayman Islands, Mauritius, Delaware LLCs) that obscure ultimate beneficial ownership and evade scrutiny  [(SEC.gov)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1880661/000188066122000008/exhibit211.htm) . The involvement of US government-backed entities like the **U.S. International Development Finance Corporation (DFC)** as limited partners in PE funds that own Indian hospitals  [(Private Equity Stakeholder Project PESP)](https://pestakeholder.org/news/private-equity-is-buying-up-hospitals-in-low-and-middle-income-countries-with-the-help-of-development-finance-institutions/)  adds a layer of official US government entanglement in Indian healthcare that has never been subject to public debate or parliamentary oversight.

### 8.5 The Path Forward: Policy Implications

The evidence leads to several policy implications. First, India needs **healthcare-specific PE regulation** that requires disclosure of ownership structures to patients, mandates minimum service obligations (including participation in public insurance schemes), and restricts practices like dividend recapitalisation that extract value from hospital assets. Second, the CCI's mandate should be expanded to include **assessment of patient welfare impacts** in healthcare M&A, not merely competition effects. Third, **DFI funding for PE-owned healthcare** should be conditioned on enforceable commitments to universal access, affordable pricing, and quality standards — with withdrawal of funding for violations. Fourth, India's **public healthcare spending must increase** from 1.2% to at least 3–5% of GDP to reduce dependence on private capital and ensure that healthcare remains a public good rather than a profit centre for global investors. Finally, the food sector requires similar attention: as PE firms acquire stakes in brands that constitute India's dietary staples, safeguards are needed to protect consumer interests, farmer livelihoods, and food security.

## 9. Conclusion: A Reckoning Is Due

The evidence assembled in this investigation is unambiguous. US private equity firms — Blackstone, KKR, Carlyle, TPG, Advent International, Warburg Pincus, Bain Capital, and others — have executed a **systematic, multi-billion-dollar acquisition strategy** across India's healthcare, pharmaceutical, and food sectors between 2020 and 2026. They have acquired controlling stakes in hospital chains serving millions of patients, pharmaceutical companies producing essential medicines, and food brands consumed daily by hundreds of millions of Indians. They have done so with **minimal regulatory scrutiny**, leveraging India's liberal FDI policies and the opacity of PE fund structures to accumulate ownership that would trigger national security reviews if conducted by state-owned enterprises.

The consequences are equally clear. Healthcare costs have **skyrocketed**, with private hospitals under PE ownership charging multiples of public facility rates. Patient complaints about **malpractice, overbilling, and denial of care** are mounting. Academic research confirms that PE ownership in healthcare is associated with **lower performance on patient outcomes, process quality, and costs**  [(HMPI)](https://hmpi.org/2024/06/19/an-update-on-impacts-of-private-equity-ownership-in-health-care-extending-a-systematic-review/) . India's out-of-pocket health expenditure remains among the world's highest, and millions of families are **pushed into poverty** annually by medical costs  [(Center for the Advanced Study of India (CASI))](https://casi.sas.upenn.edu/iit/vivek-nd-2025) . In the food sector, iconic Indian brands are being valued at **$10 billion** and sold to global investors whose fiduciary duty is to their limited partners, not to Indian consumers or farmers.

This is not an argument against foreign investment per se. India needs capital for healthcare infrastructure, pharmaceutical innovation, and food processing modernisation. But the **form and terms** of that investment matter profoundly. Portfolio investments, joint ventures with domestic partners, and greenfield developments with clear public benefit obligations are fundamentally different from leveraged buyouts that load acquired companies with debt, extract dividends through management fees, and exit within 3–7 years regardless of long-term consequences for patients, workers, and communities.

A reckoning is due. India's Parliament, regulatory agencies, civil society organisations, and — most importantly — its citizens must confront the reality that control over essential services is being transferred to global financial entities whose interests are not aligned with public welfare. The evidence has been gathered. The question is whether India will act upon it.
